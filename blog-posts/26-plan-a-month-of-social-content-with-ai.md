# How to Plan a Month of Social Content in One Sitting with AI

Most founders plan social content the same way: a burst of motivation, three posts written in one evening, then two weeks of silence. The problem isn't discipline — it's that planning a month of content from scratch is a genuinely big job, and it competes with everything else on your plate.

AI changes the economics of that job. What used to take a day of brainstorming, drafting, and formatting can now be compressed into a single focused sitting — if you use AI for the right parts and keep yourself in the loop for the parts that need judgment. Here's a workflow that does exactly that.

## Start with inputs, not prompts

The biggest mistake people make with AI content planning is opening a chat window and typing "give me 30 post ideas." You'll get thirty generic ideas that could belong to any brand.

AI output is only as specific as what you feed it. Before you generate anything, spend ten minutes gathering three things:

**Your content pillars.** The 3–5 themes your brand consistently talks about. If you haven't defined these yet, do that first — it's the single highest-leverage planning decision you can make.

**What's happening this month.** Product updates, launches, seasonal moments, events you're attending, anything with a date attached. These become anchor posts that the rest of the calendar flexes around.

**What worked recently.** Skim your last month of posts and note the two or three that got real engagement. You want the AI to build on proven ground, not start from zero.

Paste all of this into your first prompt as context. The difference in output quality is dramatic.

## Generate the skeleton, not the posts

Resist the urge to have AI write finished posts immediately. Instead, ask it to produce a month-level skeleton first: a week-by-week outline where each slot has a pillar, a format, and a one-line angle.

A good skeleton request looks like: "Using these pillars and these dated events, lay out four weeks of posts at a cadence of four per week. For each slot, give me the pillar, the format (carousel, short video, text post, image), and a one-sentence angle. Don't write the posts yet."

Working at the skeleton level has two advantages. First, it's fast to review — you can scan a month in two minutes and spot problems, like three product pitches in a row or a whole week with nothing educational. Second, edits are cheap. Swapping an angle in an outline takes seconds; rewriting a finished post you didn't want takes much longer.

Iterate on the skeleton until the shape feels right. This is where your judgment matters most, and it's the step most people skip.

## Draft in batches, one pillar at a time

Once the skeleton is approved, generate drafts — but batch them by pillar rather than going day by day. Ask for all the educational posts together, then all the product posts, then all the community or behind-the-scenes posts.

Batching by pillar keeps the AI (and you) in one mode at a time. Voice stays consistent within a theme, and you can give pillar-specific direction once instead of repeating it for every post: "educational posts should teach one specific thing and end with a question," or "product posts should show the outcome, not the feature list."

For each draft, ask for the platform-native version you actually need. A LinkedIn post, an Instagram caption, and a short-video script for the same idea are different pieces of writing — and this is exactly the kind of adaptation work AI does well and humans find tedious.

## Edit like an editor, not a writer

Now comes the part AI can't do for you. Read every draft and ask three questions:

Is it true? AI will confidently invent details, capabilities, and numbers. Anything factual — about your product, your industry, your results — needs your verification before it ships.

Does it sound like us? Fix the tells: openers you'd never use, hedging phrases, that distinctly AI-flavored enthusiasm. You usually only need to rewrite the first line and cut ten percent.

Would I stop scrolling for this? If a post is accurate and on-voice but boring, cut it or push the angle harder. A 16-post month where every post earns its place beats a 20-post month with filler.

Editing a month of drafts this way typically takes under an hour — far less than writing them from scratch, and the posts end up better than pure AI output ever is.

## Schedule everything before you stand up

The final step is the one that makes the whole exercise real: get every approved post into a scheduler with a date and time before you end the session. A month of drafts sitting in a document is a plan; a month of scheduled posts is a system.

Scheduling in the same sitting also forces the last quality check. As you slot posts into the calendar, you'll notice pacing issues — two heavy posts back to back, a launch announcement landing on a weekend — that weren't visible in a document.

## Leave room to be human

A scheduled month is a foundation, not a cage. Aim to fill about 80% of your slots in the planning session and leave the rest open for reactive content: a customer win, an industry conversation, something that made you laugh on a Tuesday.

The scheduled posts guarantee you never go silent. The open slots keep you present. Together they solve the two failure modes of social media for small teams — inconsistency and roboticness — in one afternoon a month.

That's the trade AI makes possible: you spend your limited time on judgment, taste, and truth, and let the machine handle the blank page.
