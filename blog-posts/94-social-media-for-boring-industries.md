# Your Business Isn't Boring, Your Content Is: Social Media for Unglamorous Industries

Some businesses get to post a photo of the product and call it a day. A bakery, a sneaker brand, a travel company — the thing they sell is already interesting to look at.

Then there's the rest of us. Compliance software. Freight brokerage. Dental supply. Commercial insurance. Industrial fasteners. Payroll services. You sell something that genuinely matters to the people who need it and means nothing to anyone else. Every social media guide tells you to "show your product in action," and your product in action is a spreadsheet reconciling itself.

So you post nothing for three weeks, then panic-post a stock photo with a quote on it, then post nothing again.

Here's the thing: "boring" is almost never a property of the business. It's a property of the angle you chose.

## The mistake: posting about the category instead of the problem

Most unglamorous businesses default to posting about their *category*. "Tips for better inventory management." "Why cybersecurity matters." "5 benefits of outsourced payroll."

Nobody scrolls past that and stops, because it's addressed to a category, not a person. The person reading it isn't thinking "I'd like to improve my inventory management." They're thinking "I lost $4,000 last quarter because we told a customer something was in stock and it wasn't, and I still don't know how that happened."

That second sentence is the post. The pain is specific, it has a number in it, it has a feeling in it, and anyone who has lived it will recognise themselves instantly.

The exercise: for every topic you're tempted to post about, write down the sentence a customer would actually say about it — out loud, to a friend, in their own words. Post that instead. If you've been [turning customer questions into content](/blog/turning-customer-questions-into-a-month-of-content/), you already have a file full of these.

## Five angles that work in any unglamorous niche

When the product isn't visual, the *information* has to do the work. These five angles reliably produce posts in industries where nothing photographs well.

**1. The teardown.** Take one real artifact from your world and explain it. An invoice with a line item nobody understands. A contract clause that costs people money. A shipping label. A lab report. Annotate it, explain what each part means, point at the bit where people get burned. This works because expertise looks like expertise when it's applied to something concrete.

**2. The number.** One figure, explained. The average cost of a chargeback in your category. How long a permit actually takes in your city. What a 2% error rate means in dollars at your customers' volume. Use numbers you can actually stand behind — your own data, a public source you can cite, or a figure you clearly frame as a range from your own experience. Never invent one to make a post land.

**3. The mistake.** Describe something you see customers get wrong, why it's an easy mistake, and what to do instead. This is the highest-signal content most B2B companies can make, and it's nearly free — your support inbox and your sales calls are full of it.

**4. The process, made visible.** The work you do is invisible to your customers, which is exactly why showing it is interesting. What happens between "order placed" and "it arrives"? What does your team check before signing off? Walk through it. The unglamorous middle of a process is genuinely novel to everyone outside it.

**5. The decision.** How should someone choose between two options in your space — owned vs. leased, in-house vs. outsourced, annual vs. monthly? Lay out the real trade-offs, including the cases where the answer isn't you. Honest comparisons build more trust than any amount of advocacy.

Rotate those five and you have a month of posts without ever needing a photogenic product. If you want a repeatable way to hold them, each one can become a [content pillar](/blog/content-pillars-picking-themes-your-brand-posts-about/) you keep coming back to.

## Format: words are allowed to be the content

There's a persistent belief that every post needs a designed visual. In unglamorous industries, the opposite is usually true — a plain text post, a screenshot with arrows drawn on it, or a photo of an actual whiteboard outperforms a polished template with a generic stock image behind it.

Three formats that punch above their weight here:

- **The annotated screenshot.** A real screen, with three callouts. Takes five minutes, looks like nobody else's content.
- **The plain-text breakdown.** Especially on LinkedIn and X. One claim, three short paragraphs, one takeaway.
- **The talking-head explainer.** Forty seconds of you, on your phone, answering one question a customer asked this week. No set, no script beyond a first line. If you want structure for it, the [short-form script structure](/blog/short-form-video-script-structure/) applies here too.

The photo of a handshake in front of a window is the thing that makes your content boring. Not your industry.

## Write for one person, not the trade press

A trap specific to technical and B2B founders: writing in the register of your industry's publications. Passive voice, hedged claims, nouns stacked three deep. "Operational efficiency improvements across the procurement lifecycle."

Your audience talks like a person. Write a post as if you were replying to one customer's email, then delete the greeting. That's usually the right voice — and it's the same voice that makes a [brand voice guide](/blog/brand-voice-make-ai-posts-sound-like-you/) worth writing down, so it survives when someone else drafts for you.

A quick test: read the post out loud. If you'd never say it to a customer across a table, rewrite it.

## Where AI actually helps here

Unglamorous industries are where AI assistance earns its keep, because the bottleneck isn't creativity — it's translation. You know the material cold; you just don't want to spend an hour turning what you know into five posts.

What works well: dumping a messy voice note or a long email reply into a chat and asking for three post angles from it. Taking one teardown and asking for the LinkedIn version, the X version, and the Instagram caption. Rewriting your own jargon-heavy paragraph into something a non-specialist could follow.

What doesn't work: asking a model to generate content *about* your industry from nothing. That's how you get the generic category posts we started with. The raw material has to be yours. ## Start this week

Pick one customer question from the last seven days. Write the teardown or the mistake version of it. Publish it as plain text with no visual at all.

Then do it again next week. Four of those and you'll have more useful content than most companies in your category have published all year — because they're still waiting for their product to become photogenic.
