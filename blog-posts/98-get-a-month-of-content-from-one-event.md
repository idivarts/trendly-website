# One Event, a Month of Content

Most small brands treat events as a black hole in their content calendar. You book the conference, the trade show stand, the pop-up, or the workshop. You go. You come back with 200 blurry photos, a head full of conversations, and no energy. Two weeks later you post one carousel that starts with "Thank you to everyone who stopped by!" and that's the whole return.

Meanwhile the event was the richest content opportunity of your quarter — a room full of your exact customers, real conversations, and things happening on camera. That is the raw material most founders spend the rest of the year inventing from scratch.

The fix isn't shooting more. It's deciding what you're collecting **before** you go, so capture takes five minutes a day instead of becoming a second job.

## Decide what the event is for — in content terms

Events have a business goal (leads, retail buyers, partnerships). They also have a separate content goal, and the two aren't the same. Pick one content goal before you book anything:

- **Credibility.** You want people who've never heard of you to see that serious people take you seriously. The content you need is proof of presence: the stage, the stand, the room, the people.
- **Audience growth.** You want new followers from the event's audience. The content you need is useful and public — your talk's key points, a summary of what everyone was saying, the thing nobody on stage said out loud.
- **Relationship depth.** You want the people you already know to feel closer to you. The content you need is behind the scenes: the build, the nerves, the 6am setup, the thing that broke.

One of them decides what you shoot. Credibility content and behind-the-scenes content look nothing alike, and trying to get both on the day is how people end up with neither.

## Build the calendar before you pack

Here's the structure that works, mapped to a single event date:

**Two weeks out — anticipation.** One post that says you're going, who you want to meet, and where to find you. This is the post that gets you meetings, not likes. Name the hall, the booth number, the day you're speaking. Make it easy for someone to reply "I'll be there."

**One week out — the useful pre-post.** Something genuinely helpful tied to the event's theme: what you expect to see, the three questions you're going there to answer, your honest take on the industry conversation. This is the piece that reaches people who aren't attending, which is most of your audience.

**The day before — logistics plus personality.** Packing, printing, the last-minute reprint because the signage was wrong. Cheap to make, surprisingly well-watched.

**Event days — one post per day, maximum.** Not ten. One.

**Day after — the fast recap.** Within 24 hours, while anyone who was there is still scrolling for event content. Don't wait for the perfect edit.

**Week after — the substance.** This is where the real value lands: what you actually learned, the pattern across every conversation you had, the thing you're changing because of it.

**Two to four weeks after — the long tail.** Quote cards from your talk, the clip from the panel, the single slide people photographed, answers to the question five different people asked you.

That's nine to twelve posts from one event, and only three of them require you to make anything while you're exhausted. Put the whole run into your [content calendar](/product/content-calendar) before you leave, with the post-event slots blocked out as placeholders. Empty slots with a date and a label get filled; vague intentions don't. If you've never set this up, our guide to [building a content calendar you'll actually stick to](/blog/content-calendar-you-will-actually-stick-to) covers the mechanics.

## The capture list: five minutes a day, not a second job

Write this list in your notes app before you travel. On the day, you're not making decisions, you're ticking boxes.

1. **One wide shot of the room or stand.** Proof of scale. Shoot it early before it gets messy.
2. **One shot of you in it.** Ask someone. This is the single asset you will miss most if you skip it, and the one founders always skip.
3. **Three to five vertical clips, 10 seconds each.** Walking in, the stand from the aisle, something being demoed, the hall emptying out. Vertical, held steady, no narration needed.
4. **One photo of something written.** A slide, a whiteboard, a sign, a stat on screen. Screenshot-able content travels further than your face does.
5. **Voice notes, not captions.** After every real conversation, record 20 seconds into your phone: what they asked, what surprised you. This is the highest-value thing you'll collect all week and it takes no effort. It's also what makes the week-after post actually good instead of generic.
6. **Three quotes you overheard.** Anonymised. "Three people at this event told me X" is a better post than any photo.

Six items. If you get four of them, the month is covered.

Dump everything into one place the same night — a single folder per event, named with the date. Travel content is the easiest content to lose, and a post you can't find the footage for is a post that doesn't happen. If your files live in six different apps, our [content asset library](/blog/content-asset-library-for-social-media) guide is worth reading.

## Turn the raw material into posts, not a highlight reel

The mistake is treating all of it as one big recap. Split it by what each piece does:

- **The voice notes become the thinking posts.** "I had the same conversation nine times this week" is a stronger opening than any photo. Transcribe them and you have the week-after post, plus two or three spin-offs.
- **The clips become the recap video.** Three to five ten-second clips, cut together, text on screen. Twenty minutes of work, not an afternoon.
- **The written thing becomes the carousel.** One stat or slide, your take on it, why it matters for someone like your reader.
- **The quotes become standalone posts.** One per week for the next month. Each one is two sentences and needs no design.
- **The wide shot becomes the credibility post.** This is the one you pin, and the one you reuse next year when you announce you're going again.

This is the same repurposing logic that works on any single asset — if you want the full version of the method, [one reel, five posts](/blog/repurpose-one-reel-into-five-pieces-of-content) breaks it down step by step.

## Protect the days you're away

The reason most event content never ships is that the week of the event is the week you have the least capacity. So don't plan to post live. Schedule the pre-event posts a week ahead. During the event you are capturing only, plus one quick daily post if you have it in you. The recap and everything after it gets scheduled on the flight home or the morning after, when you can see the material and think clearly.

Your regular content shouldn't stop dead for ten days either. Queue your normal cadence through the event window before you travel, the same way you'd prepare for [any time away](/blog/take-time-off-without-going-quiet-on-social). An audience that goes quiet for two weeks loses more reach than the event gains you.

## If you're on a team, assign capture to a person

In a two or three person team, "everyone take photos" means nobody does. Name one person as capture owner for the event — not the person running the booth or giving the talk, because they physically can't. Their job is the six-item list, nothing else. Everyone else forwards voice notes to them at the end of each day.

Then make sure the recap doesn't sit in a draft for a week waiting for approval. Agree before you go who signs off on event content and how fast, so the day-after post goes out on the day after. Fast-moving content needs a fast lane; the rest of your [approval workflow](/blog/content-approval-workflow-small-teams) can stay as it is.

## The real test

A month after the event, look at your feed. If the only trace is one thank-you post, you bought a booth and threw the content away. If there are eight to ten posts, half of them useful to people who never attended, you got the event and a month of your calendar for the price of one trip.

The planning that gets you there takes about thirty minutes, and all of it happens before you leave.
