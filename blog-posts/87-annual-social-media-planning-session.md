# The Annual Planning Session: How to Map Next Year's Social Before January

It's late October, which means somewhere in your inbox there's a template called "2027 Social Media Strategy" with forty slides and a RACI chart. Ignore it. You are not staffing a brand team of twelve, and you cannot write next year's captions this afternoon.

But there is a version of annual planning that genuinely helps a founder or a two-person team, and it takes about three hours. It doesn't produce a calendar. It produces a small set of decisions you'd otherwise re-make, badly, under pressure, forty times over the next twelve months.

Here's what that session looks like.

## Why most annual plans die in February

Annual social plans fail for a predictable reason: they try to pre-decide the wrong layer. Someone sits down in December and specifies posts — the January 14 carousel, the February customer story — and then the product slips, a competitor launches, a video takes off unexpectedly, and the document becomes a monument to who you were last quarter.

Specific posts have a shelf life of about two weeks. Direction has a shelf life of a year. The whole trick of annual planning is to only commit to the things that are still going to be true in September.

So your output isn't a calendar. It's four decisions: what the year is *for*, which fixed moments you already know about, which channels you're actually betting on, and the cadence you can hold in your worst month. Everything downstream — themes, briefs, individual posts — gets planned monthly and weekly, where it belongs.

## Hour one: look back before you look forward

Open your analytics for the last twelve months. Not to make a report — to answer four questions, each with a written sentence:

**Which five posts performed best, and what did they have in common?** Look past the topic at the *form*: were they teardowns, before-and-afters, opinions, behind-the-scenes, customer wins? You're hunting for a repeatable shape, not a one-off hit. If you're unsure which numbers to trust here, the [metrics that actually matter](/blog/social-metrics-that-actually-move-the-needle) are saves, shares, and profile visits — not likes.

**Which five performed worst?** Usually there's a category you keep publishing out of obligation. Name it. You are allowed to stop.

**What did you publish consistently, and what did you abandon?** The format you quietly dropped in March is telling you something about your capacity, not your taste.

**What actually produced business outcomes** — a signup, a demo, a sale, a useful conversation in the DMs? That's often a completely different list from the top-performing one, and it deserves disproportionate weight next year.

By the end of the hour you should have one page of findings — the only input to this session that's grounded in evidence rather than ambition.

## Hour two, part one: pick three anchors

An anchor is a sentence describing what the year is for, tied to the business rather than the feed. Three is the right number — one is too vague to guide anything, five means you've just written a wish list.

Good anchors sound like:

- *Become the obvious choice for agencies, not just solo users.*
- *Make the product's hardest concept legible before the pricing change in Q3.*
- *Build an owned audience so we're less dependent on reach.*

Bad anchors sound like *grow the Instagram following* or *post more consistently*. Those are inputs. They belong in cadence, further down.

Each anchor should imply content. "Become the obvious choice for agencies" means more multi-client workflow posts, more operational detail, probably more LinkedIn. That implication is the actual value of the exercise — when you're staring at an empty week in May, the anchors tell you what kind of post is worth making.

This is also where your [content pillars](/blog/content-pillars-picking-themes-your-brand-posts-about) get a yearly sanity check. Pillars are the standing categories; anchors are what you're pushing this year. If none of your pillars serve any anchor, one of the two is wrong.

## Hour two, part two: map the fixed moments

Now make twelve rows — one per month — and fill in only what you already know:

- **Product moments.** Launches, pricing changes, a beta opening, a big feature. Put them in even if the dates are soft; a question mark is more useful than a blank.
- **Business moments.** Conferences, funding news, a hiring push, your company's anniversary.
- **Calendar moments that genuinely fit you.** Black Friday if you sell something, tax season if you're in finance, back-to-school if you serve teachers. Most national-day hashtags fit nobody; the [seasonal mapping approach](/blog/planning-content-around-seasonal-moments) is about tiering these honestly rather than filling every square.
- **Your own quiet months.** If you take two weeks off in August, write it down now. That's the single most useful thing on the sheet, because it's the month that needs content banked in advance.

Twelve rows with three or four entries each is a complete annual map. Most months will be nearly empty, and that's correct — empty months are where you'll react to whatever's actually happening. A month with a known launch becomes a [monthly theme](/blog/monthly-content-themes-give-each-month-a-focus) almost by itself.

## Hour three, part one: make the channel bet explicit

Every small team is on more platforms than it can serve. Annual planning is the moment to say so out loud and rank them: one primary channel that gets original thinking, one or two secondary channels that get adapted versions, and a list of accounts you're keeping alive without pretending to grow them.

Write the demotion down. Unwritten demotions turn into guilt, and guilt produces the worst content you'll publish all year. If the ranking isn't obvious, run through the [platform decision framework](/blog/which-social-platforms-should-your-brand-be-on) before you commit — and revisit it next year rather than mid-March.

## Hour three, part two: set the cadence you can survive in your worst month

Here's the rule that saves annual plans: pick the volume you could maintain during your single busiest, most chaotic month — then plan the year at that number.

If that's three posts a week on one channel, plan three. You will have good months where you publish more, and that upside costs you nothing. What costs you is a plan built for your best week, which quietly becomes a weekly reminder that you're behind. Realistic volume is the whole argument behind [a cadence that doesn't burn you out](/blog/consistent-posting-cadence-without-burnout) and the reason to keep [your goals deliberately small](/blog/setting-realistic-social-media-goals-small-brand).

Then multiply: three a week is roughly 150 posts a year. Knowing the real number makes the case for [recycling what already worked](/blog/recycle-best-performing-content) instead of writing 150 originals.

## Leave 30% of the year deliberately empty

Reserve roughly a third of your planned slots with no topic attached. Those slots are for trends you can't predict, customer questions you haven't been asked yet, the competitor move you'll want to answer, and the post you'll want to make the day after something goes well.

A plan with no slack isn't a plan, it's a commitment to ignore reality. Teams that fill every square in December end up either abandoning the plan or publishing content that was interesting ten months ago.

## The quarterly check-in that keeps it alive

Put four 45-minute reviews on the calendar now — one per quarter — with three questions: Are the anchors still right? What did the last quarter's numbers change about our minds? What's in the next quarter's fixed-moment row that needs content banked early?

That's the entire maintenance cost. Four short meetings, and the plan stays connected to what's actually happening instead of becoming archaeology.

## Where the tooling actually helps

Once the decisions exist, the mechanical part gets much faster. If your anchors, pillars, and voice live in a [content strategy your AI workspace can read](/product/ai-strategy), expanding "October: agency workflows" into a month of briefs and drafts becomes a prompt rather than a blank page — and the output arrives aimed at this year's anchors instead of generic best practice. Push those into [a calendar you plan and publish from](/product/content-calendar) and the gap between "we decided this in October" and "it's scheduled" stops being where plans go to die.

What no tool can do is make the four decisions. That's the three hours. Block them this week — while the year is still fresh enough to learn from, and far enough from January that you're not planning in a panic.
