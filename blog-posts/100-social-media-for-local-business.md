# Your Audience Is Ten Minutes Away: Social Media for Businesses With a Front Door

Most social media advice is written for businesses that can sell to anyone, anywhere: grow the audience, widen the funnel, chase reach.

If you run a café, a dental practice, a pilates studio, a barbershop or a repair shop, that advice is quietly working against you. A post that reaches 40,000 people in another country is a worse outcome than one that reaches 400 people who could walk in on Saturday. Reach is not your metric. **Radius is** — and that one change rewrites almost every decision below.

## Stop reading your reach number. Start reading your radius.

The follower count on a local account is a vanity number with a twist: it can be *actively misleading*. One viral post brings thousands of followers who will never visit, and from then on your engagement rate looks broken and your audience insights point at the wrong people.

Judge your content on signals only a local can produce:

- **Saves and shares** — someone sending your post to a friend they'd actually meet there
- **Direction taps, map clicks and profile link clicks** — intent to arrive
- **DMs asking practical questions** — hours, parking, availability, price
- **Followers from your own city**, which most audience insights break out by location
- **"I saw your post" at the counter** — unmeasurable in-app, and the truest signal you have

[The social metrics that actually move the needle](/blog/social-metrics-that-actually-move-the-needle) covers which numbers to ignore in general. Locally, add one filter to all of it: *could this person get here?*

## Pick fewer platforms, and pick them for local behaviour

National brands spread across six channels. You should not. Choose by how people near you actually look for what you sell:

- **Instagram** — anything visual and walk-in: food, hair, interiors, retail, fitness. Also where people check you look open and alive before visiting.
- **Facebook** — still the strongest local channel in many markets, because of groups and events. Don't skip it for feeling unfashionable; check whether your town's groups are busy.
- **Google Business Profile** — not social media, but it belongs in the content plan. Photos, posts and reviews here decide whether anyone finds you at all.
- **TikTok** — worth it if your category has an "I didn't know this was here" factor, and most views being out of town doesn't bother you.

Two channels done properly beats five done thinly. Our [platform decision framework](/blog/which-social-platforms-should-your-brand-be-on) covers how to choose and, more importantly, what to drop.

## Five post types that do the work locally

Local content doesn't need to be clever. It needs to answer what a nearby stranger is quietly asking.

**1. The "what it's like inside" post.** People hesitate before entering unfamiliar places. Show the room, the counter, the chair, the parking, the entrance from the street. The most undervalued local post there is, because it removes a real barrier to arriving.

**2. The logistics post.** Holiday hours, where to park, which door to use, whether you take walk-ins, how long the Saturday wait is. Boring to write, endlessly saved.

**3. The person-behind-the-counter post.** Local businesses win on familiarity. Introduce the team by name — people come back for a person more readily than for a brand.

**4. The neighbourhood post.** Your street, the market down the road, the event this weekend. You're part of a place, and these reach locals browsing local tags.

**5. The proof post.** A regular's order, a before/after, a review screenshot with permission. Your customers already produce this — [a UGC system](/blog/ugc-system-customer-content-small-brands) makes it repeatable instead of accidental.

None of these need trend research. They need attention to what happens in your own building.

## Get found by people searching nearby

People increasingly search *inside* social apps the way they search Google — an opportunity national competitors handle worse than you can.

- **Put your city or neighbourhood in the profile name field**, not just the bio — it's indexed in in-app search. "Mera Coffee — Brixton" beats "Mera Coffee".
- **Tag your location on every post**, plus the venue, street or district. Location pages get browsed by people planning an afternoon.
- **Use a few genuinely local hashtags** alongside category ones. Local tags have small audiences, which is the point.
- **Write captions the way people search.** "Best flat white near Brixton station" belongs in a sentence, not just a tag.
- **Fix the bio first** — it's the page every new local visitor lands on ([the bio guide](/blog/social-media-profile-bio-optimization) covers the first two lines).

[Social SEO](/blog/social-seo-get-found-in-app-search) goes deeper; the local version is simply: name the place, everywhere, in plain language.

## A weekly rhythm you can run between customers

The hardest part of local social isn't ideas — it's that you're on the floor all day. The plan has to survive a busy Saturday.

- **Three posts a week** — one practical, one human, one proof or neighbourhood.
- **Stories or short posts when it's natural** — what's on today, what just came in, what's nearly sold out. Thirty seconds, from the phone, no production.
- **One planning session a week.** Thirty to sixty minutes: draft the three, schedule them, done.
- **One review ask a week.** Not social, but the highest-leverage local marketing action there is.

Capture is the trick. Photograph the place while it looks good, not when you need a post — a few minutes after opening, before the first rush. Three usable photos a week is a library by the end of the quarter.

## Two advantages a national brand cannot copy

**You can be in the same room as your audience.** A workshop, a tasting, a market stall, a charity collection: one afternoon generates weeks of content, and the people in the photos are the people who'll see them — [one event, a month of content](/blog/get-a-month-of-content-from-one-event) breaks that down.

**You have neighbours.** The florist, the gym, the barber two doors down all have an audience that overlaps yours for free, and none of you compete. A swap — a post each, a shared offer, a joint event — is the cheapest local growth there is ([the partnership playbook](/blog/brand-partnership-cross-promotion-small-brands) covers setting one up).

## Treat your DMs like your phone line

A local DM is rarely a comment — it's someone asking whether they can come in today. Hours, availability, "do you have this in stock", "can you fit me in at 4". These are sales enquiries with a short shelf life: an unanswered one on Friday afternoon is a lost Saturday booking.

Pick a response window you can hold (same day is fine), save the three or four replies you type constantly, and make sure someone other than you can see the inbox.

## The local playbook, condensed

- Judge posts by saves, shares, direction taps and local followers — not reach
- Pick two channels, plus your Google Business Profile
- Put your city in the profile name, location-tag every post, write searchable captions
- Rotate practical / human / proof posts, three a week
- Photograph the place when it looks good, not when you need a post
- Treat DMs as a phone line with a response window
- Trade audiences with the businesses around you, and ask for one review a week

Local social is less about marketing talent than about consistency under operational pressure. The plan loses to the lunch rush unless the posting is already scheduled before the week starts.
