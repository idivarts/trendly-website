# That Account Isn't You: Handling Fake Profiles and Brand Impersonation

It usually arrives as a screenshot. A customer sends you a DM they received — "Congratulations, you've been selected!" — from an account with your logo, your bio, and your handle with one extra underscore in it. They want to know if it's real. Sometimes they've already paid a "delivery fee."

This is one of the few social media problems where the cost lands on someone else before it lands on you. Your follower is out money and trust; you find out second-hand. And because it's nobody's job until it happens, most small teams spend the first hour deciding what to do instead of doing it. Here's the version worth having written down before you need it.

## Three different problems get called "a fake account"

They need different responses, so separate them first.

**The impostor.** Mimics your name, handle, photo and bio closely enough to fool a fast scroll, then DMs your followers — a prize to claim, an order to confirm, a payment link, a "verification" request. This is the urgent one. It has a clock on it because every hour it stays up is more people contacted.

**The copycat.** Reposts your photos, your captions, sometimes your whole feed, under a different name. They're not pretending to be you; they're using your work. Worth acting on, but it's an intellectual-property question, not a safety one.

**The parked handle.** An inactive account sitting on a name close to yours, or one you used to have. No activity, no harm being done — lowest priority.

If you only have time for one, it's always the impostor. Nobody's bank account is harmed by a copycat.

## The first hour, in order: document, report, warn

The order matters more than the speed.

**Document before you report.** Screenshot the full profile — handle, display name, follower count, bio, post grid — plus the URL, the forwarded DMs and the date. Accounts disappear when they're reported, and your evidence goes with them. If you later need to show a pattern, or hand something to a bank or a lawyer, this is the only record that will exist.

**Report as impersonation, not as spam.** Platforms route these to different queues, and the generic spam path is the slower one. Report from your official account so the reviewer sees the real thing beside the fake, then ask a handful of people — your team, the customers who flagged it — to report it too. A few corroborating reports help; a mass-identical brigade can read as coordinated abuse.

**Warn your audience where they'll actually see it.** A story, plus one pinned post or comment. Keep it short, calm and factual:

> There's an account impersonating us and messaging people about prizes. Our only account is @ourrealhandle. We will never ask you for payment, card details, or a fee to release anything. If you get a message like that, don't reply — report it.

That's the whole announcement. Resist the urge to write a thread about it. The goal is a sentence people can remember, not a statement.

## What not to say

**Don't tag the fake account.** Tagging sends your audience to it, which is what it wants, and it hands a real human — who may be the wrong one — a pile-on from your followers. Describe it; don't link it.

**Don't go quiet and hope.** The fake account is working right now, in a channel you can't see. One clear note is cheaper than ten apologetic replies later.

The same instinct applies here as in any bad moment: tone is the whole job. Our [crisis plan post](/blog/social-media-crisis-plan-small-brands/) covers how to say something quickly without making the thing bigger than it is.

## Reporting that actually goes somewhere

The in-app report button is the fastest path and worth using first. For anything persistent, platforms also run dedicated forms for business impersonation and intellectual-property claims, handled by a slower but more serious queue. Have these ready, because the forms ask for them:

- Your official account handle and URL.
- The infringing account's handle and URL, plus specific post links.
- Proof you represent the business: your website on your own domain, company registration details, and a trademark registration number if you have one.
- A clear statement of what's being misused — your name, your logo, your photos — and what you want done.

**Trademark registration is the single biggest difference** between a case that resolves in days and one that sits for weeks: claims backed by a registration get processed on a stronger track than "this is my name." If your brand name is the business, registering it in your main market is the cheapest insurance in this entire post.

File once, record the reference number, follow up once. Re-filing the same claim from five accounts resets your queue position more often than it speeds anything up.

## Make your real account harder to fake

Most of what makes impersonation easy is on your side of the fence.

- **Two-factor authentication with an authenticator app**, not SMS, on every account — and a recovery email that isn't a shared team inbox.
- **Own accounts at the business level, not from somebody's personal profile.** If your pages hang off one founder's login, you have a bigger problem than a copycat. The [account access checklist](/blog/who-owns-your-social-accounts-access-checklist/) walks through the fix.
- **Claim your handle on platforms you don't use yet.** It's free, and a parked official account can't be taken by anyone else.
- **Take verification where it's available to you.** A badge isn't a status symbol here — it's a two-second distinguisher for a customer deciding which of two accounts is real.
- **Write your real handle out in full** on your website, email footer and packaging. The best defense against a lookalike is an audience who knows the exact spelling.

## Someone is reposting your content, not your identity

If the issue is stolen posts rather than a stolen name, you have a copyright route: a takedown request for your own photos and video through the platform's IP form. It works, it's reasonably quick, and a clear-cut case doesn't need a lawyer.

What's worth deciding honestly is whether to spend the hours. A dormant account recycling your reels from a year ago is noise. One actively selling to your market under your brand's look is worth the paperwork. Everything in between is a judgment call, and "out-publish them" is a legitimate answer.

## Catch it earlier, by actually reading the inbox

Impersonation is almost never discovered by the brand. It's discovered by a customer replying to a suspicious DM in your real inbox, or leaving a confused comment under a post — which means it's found only if someone reads comments and messages as a routine, the case we made in [one inbox, every platform](/blog/unified-inbox-social-comments-dms-workflow/). Check DMs weekly and you find out about this a week late.

Two habits close the gap: search your brand name on each platform once a month and glance at the accounts using it, and set up a name alert so mentions outside your own comments reach you — the routine in our [social listening post](/blog/social-listening-routine-small-brands/).

## Write the two-paragraph runbook now

The real fix is taking the decisions out of the moment. On a calm afternoon, settle four things: who screenshots and files the report, who posts the warning, where the evidence folder lives, and the exact sentence you'll publish. Save that warning as a draft rather than trusting you'll remember it — the version you write unhurried today beats the one you'd type at 9pm.
