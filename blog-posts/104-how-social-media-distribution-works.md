# Who Actually Sees Your Post? A Plain-English Guide to Social Distribution

Most founders treat the algorithm like weather: unknowable, occasionally cruel, best complained about. So they optimise for the things they can see — post times, hashtag counts, follower totals — and stay confused when a post they loved goes nowhere and a throwaway one takes off.

The useful mental model is simpler than it looks. No platform publishes its ranking formula, and the details differ between Instagram, TikTok, LinkedIn and X. But the shape is remarkably consistent, and once you can see the shape, most "algorithm" questions answer themselves.

## Nobody is hiding your posts from you

Start by throwing out the suppression story. Your reach isn't being withheld as punishment for not paying for ads, and there is no secret list your account is on.

What's actually happening is a ranking problem. Every platform has far more content than attention to spend on it. Someone who follows 800 accounts cannot be shown 800 accounts' worth of posts, so the feed is a ranked selection, not a chronological log. Your post isn't competing against a quota. It's competing for a slot against everything else that person might plausibly want to see — including content from accounts they don't follow at all.

That reframing matters. "How do I beat the algorithm?" has no answer. "Why would this particular person stop scrolling for this?" has several.

## Stage one: the small first audience

Almost every platform distributes in stages. When you publish, the post goes to a relatively small slice of people most likely to care — usually some mix of recent engagers, close connections, and followers who are active right now.

This first slice is a test, and it's small enough that normal variance swings it hard. The same post published on a day when your most engaged followers happen to be busy will open weaker than it would have on another day. This is most of what people experience as "the algorithm changed this week." A handful of posts is a sample size of a handful.

What you control here is honest: whether the first moment of the post — the first line, the first frame, the cover image — gives that small audience a reason to stop. Everything downstream depends on this stage going reasonably well, which is why hooks get so much attention. It's not a trick. It's the only part of the post the test audience sees before deciding.

## Stage two: the signals that buy more reach

If the first slice responds, the post gets shown to a wider one, and the cycle repeats. What counts as "responds" is where people go wrong, because the loudest metric — likes — is close to the weakest signal.

Think about it from the platform's side. It wants to predict whether the *next* person will find the post worth their time. So the behaviours it leans on are the ones that cost the viewer something or reveal real intent:

- **Watch time and completion.** On video, how far people get is the strongest available proxy for value. A fifteen-second clip watched to the end can out-travel a sixty-second one abandoned at eight seconds.
- **Saves and shares.** Saving says "I want this later." Sharing says "I'll spend my own reputation on this." Both are much harder to get than a like, which is exactly why they carry weight.
- **Comments and replies, especially conversational ones.** A thread of actual back-and-forth signals something a row of fire emojis doesn't.
- **Dwell time.** How long someone lingers, even without tapping anything — on a carousel, a long caption, a text post.
- **Profile taps and follows.** The clearest signal of all: this post made someone want more of you.

Likes still count, but they're cheap to give and cheap to predict. If you want distribution, aim at the expensive signals.

## Why engagement bait backfires

Once you know the signals, the temptation is to farm them: "comment YES below," "save this for later," follow-for-follow, a reply thread of one-word answers. Platforms have been penalising obvious bait for years, and even where it isn't penalised, it decays fast — you train an audience to perform a gesture rather than to want the thing.

The durable version of the same move is to earn the signal. A post worth saving is one with something specific in it — a checklist, a number, a script someone will need next Tuesday. A post worth sharing either says something the sharer wishes they'd said, or is useful to a particular someone they can tag. You're not gaming a metric. You're making the thing the metric is trying to detect.

## What this actually changes about your content

Three practical shifts come out of this model.

**Design for one signal per post.** Before you publish, answer: what is this post *for*? Saved, shared, replied to, watched through, clicked? A post built to be saved looks different from one built to start an argument. Posts that chase all five tend to land none.

**Judge posts by rate, not totals.** Reach is partly a function of the first slice's mood on the day. Saves per reach, completion rate, replies per reach — these tell you whether the post worked. Raw reach tells you how big the test was. If you only track totals, you'll keep rewarding the wrong posts.

**Give the pattern time.** Any single post is noise. Ten posts of the same format is data. This is the real argument for a consistent cadence: not that the platform rewards frequency for its own sake, but that you can't learn anything from a sample of two. (More on that in [how often you should post on each platform](/blog/posting-frequency-guide-per-platform/).)

## Where format fits

Formats don't have magic reach. What they have is different signal profiles. Short video is built to generate watch time and reach non-followers. Carousels are built to generate dwell and saves. Text posts on LinkedIn and X are built for comments. Stories barely travel at all, but they're strong at depth with people who already know you.

So "should we do more Reels?" isn't really a format question. It's a question about which signal you most need right now — new reach, or depth with the audience you already have. Both are valid, and they need different posts.

## The part worth more than algorithm literacy

Here's the honest ceiling on all of this: understanding distribution gets you from guessing to reasoning. It doesn't substitute for having something to say, or for showing up long enough to be recognised.

The highest-leverage work is still upstream — knowing which few themes you're credible on, building a planning habit you can sustain, and reviewing what actually worked often enough to adjust.
