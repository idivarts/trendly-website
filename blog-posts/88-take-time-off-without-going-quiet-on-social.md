# How to Take Two Weeks Off Without Going Quiet on Social

Every founder who runs their own social hits the same wall before a holiday: the account only works because you're at the keyboard. Take ten days off and either the feed goes silent, or you spend the whole break answering DMs from a phone at the dinner table.

Both options are bad, and the usual advice — "just schedule some posts" — solves a quarter of the problem. Publishing is the easy part. What actually breaks while you're away is everything nobody pre-decided: a scheduled post landing on a day that turned out to be wrong, a sales question sitting unread for nine days, a complaint escalating because nobody had permission to answer it.

Here's a plan that takes about two hours to set up and holds for a couple of weeks.

## First, decide what "covered" actually means

Before you schedule anything, pick one of three levels honestly. Most people fail by assuming the first and delivering the third.

**Fully active.** Posts go out, comments and DMs get answered within a day, and someone can react to things that happen. This requires a second person. Not a tool — a person.

**Maintenance.** Posts go out on a pre-approved queue. Someone checks the inbox once a day for ten minutes and answers only the easy, high-value messages. Everything else waits.

**Deliberately quiet.** You publish nothing, say so in advance, and come back on a stated date.

Maintenance is the right answer for most solo founders and two-person teams. Fully active is what you'll *tell* yourself you're doing, and it's how holidays get ruined. Build for maintenance properly and you'll come back to an account that's intact rather than one that's been half-watched from a phone.

## Bank the queue two weeks out, not the night before

Start a fortnight ahead, because the useful work isn't writing — it's choosing what's safe to publish unattended.

Fill the queue at your *lower* cadence, not your usual one. If you normally post four times a week, schedule two or three. The honest version of your [sustainable posting cadence](/blog/consistent-posting-cadence-without-burnout) drops while you're away, and nobody is counting.

Lean on content that doesn't age — educational posts, how-tos, answers to common customer questions. [Recycling what already worked](/blog/recycle-best-performing-content) is never more defensible than during a break, because there's nobody around to make something new.

And get every asset in place before you leave. A queue that depends on "I'll add the photo later" is a queue that breaks on day three. This is where a real [content asset library](/blog/content-asset-library-for-social-media) earns its keep.

## What should never sit on an unattended queue

This is the list most people skip, and it's the one that causes the actual damage.

- **Anything time-sensitive.** A sale that might sell out, a webinar that might be rescheduled, a "last chance" that depends on a date you can't verify from a plane.
- **Anything reactive.** A take on a trend, a response to a competitor, a comment on a news story. By the time it publishes, the context has moved.
- **Anything that needs a working link.** If a landing page changes while you're away, a scheduled post becomes a broken promise.
- **Anything launch-adjacent.** Launches need someone watching the replies. Move the whole [launch sequence](/blog/plan-a-product-launch-across-social-channels) either side of the trip.
- **Jokes.** Humor that reads fine today reads badly against news you can't predict. This is the specific failure mode that makes a quiet account look callous.

If a post survives that filter, it's safe to leave running.

## Know where the pause button is — before you need it

The one control that matters while you're away is the ability to stop everything at once. If something happens in the world, or to your company, the right first move is almost always to pause the entire queue and decide afterwards. That's the core of any workable [crisis plan](/blog/social-media-crisis-plan-small-brands), and it is far easier to use if you've found the control once while calm.

Make sure whoever is covering can reach it too. A deputy who can't pause the queue isn't covering; they're watching.

## The inbox is the part everyone forgets

A silent feed is survivable. An unanswered buying question for nine days is lost revenue, and an unanswered complaint compounds.

Pick one of these and commit:

**A named deputy.** Someone who checks comments and DMs daily. Give them five or six saved replies covering the questions you get most — pricing, shipping, "do you do X", "how do I get started" — plus one escalation rule: *anything you're unsure about, screenshot it and leave it for me.* The handoff mechanics are the same ones that make [delegating social work](/blog/delegate-social-media-without-losing-brand-voice) survivable at any time.

**A daily ten-minute pass.** If you're genuinely solo, do it once, at a fixed time, from a [single inbox that shows every platform](/blog/unified-inbox-social-comments-dms-workflow). Ten minutes at 9am is sustainable. Checking your phone all day is not — and it's the thing that turns a holiday into a half-worked fortnight.

Either way, set expectations publicly. An honest line in your bio or an auto-reply — "replies are slower until the 6th" — converts a slow response from a red flag into a known condition.

## Write the away sheet

One page, shared with whoever is covering, written before you leave:

1. **Who decides** if something unexpected happens. One name, not a committee.
2. **How to pause the queue** — the literal steps.
3. **A holding line** you've pre-approved, for buying time: *"Thanks for flagging this — we're looking into it and will come back to you."*
4. **The escalation threshold.** What's worth messaging you about, and what definitively isn't.
5. **Your actual availability.** "Reachable for anything in point 4, otherwise offline" beats a vague promise to check in.

Five bullets. It's the difference between a deputy who acts and a deputy who freezes.

## Going quiet on purpose is a legitimate choice

If you can't staff maintenance, don't fake it. Publish one post before you leave that says you're taking a break and when you're back, then stop.

Audiences forgive an announced absence easily — what reads badly is an account that trails off mid-thought and reappears without explanation. A stated return date also does something useful for you: it turns coming back into a scheduled commitment instead of a vague intention you keep deferring.

## Give yourself a re-entry window

Don't resume at full speed. Block thirty minutes on your first morning back and do three things in order: read the inbox before you post anything, check what actually published, and note anything that happened you'd want to respond to now.

The post you planned for day one is rarely the right post once you've read the two weeks you missed.

## Where the setup pays for itself

Most of this is just [scheduling ahead](/blog/why-scheduling-ahead-reduces-stress-improves-quality) with the edge cases handled, and it's far less work when the queue, the assets, and the inbox live in one place: you fill [the calendar](/product/content-calendar) with pre-approved posts, your deputy sees exactly what's going out and when, and every platform's comments and DMs land in one queue someone can clear in ten minutes.

Block the two hours a fortnight before you go. A break you've planned for is the only kind you come back from rested — and the only kind your account survives unchanged.
