# Borrow Each Other's Audience: A Brand Partnership Playbook for Small Brands

Most growth advice assumes you're talking to people who already follow you. Post more. Post better. Post at the right time. All of it is optimization inside a room you've already filled.

The harder problem is getting in front of people who have never heard of you. There are three usual answers: pay for ads, pay a creator, or wait for the algorithm to pick you. There's a fourth that small brands routinely skip because it feels like asking a favour — find another brand your ideal customer already trusts, and do something together.

It costs nothing but coordination. And unlike a cold ad impression, you arrive pre-vouched-for.

## What actually makes a good partner

The instinct is to aim up — pitch the biggest account in your category and hope. That almost never lands, and when it does the terms are lopsided. The partnerships that work share four traits:

**Complementary, not competing.** You want the brand that sells the thing your customer buys *next to* yours, not instead of it. A ceramics studio and a specialty coffee roaster. A bookkeeping service and a business-insurance broker. A yoga studio and a smoothie bar. Nobody has to lose for the other to win.

**Roughly the same size.** Not identical, but close enough that both sides are giving up something real. If one audience is ten times the other, the smaller brand is asking for a donation — and the pitch will read that way no matter how it's worded.

**The same actual audience.** Follower count is the easy check; audience *overlap* is the one that matters. A brand can be your size and your category and still be talking to a different person — different price point, different city, different life stage. Scroll their comments. If you don't recognise the people, your post won't land there either.

**Visibly alive.** They post consistently, they reply to comments, somebody is clearly home. A partnership with a dormant account is a post into a void.

## Where to find them

You almost certainly already know three candidates.

Start with your own following list and your tagged photos — brands your customers mention, brands you buy from, brands that have liked your posts. Then look at who your most engaged followers follow. If you've ever done a [competitor content audit](/blog/competitor-content-audit-what-to-actually-learn), you built half this list already; the difference is you're now looking one shelf over instead of straight at your rivals.

Local is underrated. Two small businesses in the same neighbourhood have an obvious shared story, an easy excuse to be in the same room, and an audience that overlaps for free.

## Five formats worth doing

Partnerships die from vagueness. "Let's collaborate" is not a plan. Pick a shape before you reach out:

1. **A genuine collab post.** Instagram's collaborator feature puts one post on both profiles and both feeds, with shared engagement. It's the lowest-effort, highest-reach option available, and it's underused.
2. **A story or feed takeover swap.** You make a piece of content for their audience, they make one for yours. Each side is introducing a trusted friend, not advertising.
3. **A joint giveaway.** Two prizes, two audiences, one entry. The usual [giveaway rules](/blog/how-to-run-a-social-media-giveaway) still apply — especially the part about a prize only your real customer wants, which matters double when you're splitting the cost.
4. **A bundle or paired offer.** Buy from one, get something from the other. This is the only format on the list that produces revenue you can actually count, which makes it the easiest to justify repeating.
5. **A list or community swap.** A mention in each other's newsletter, community, or Slack. Smaller numbers, far warmer than a feed impression.

The first three take an afternoon each. Start there.

## The message that gets a yes

Most partnership pitches fail for the same reason most cold emails fail: they ask the recipient to do the thinking.

Don't send "love your brand, would you want to collaborate sometime?" Send the finished idea. Name the format, the rough date, exactly what you'll make, exactly what you're asking them to make, and why their audience benefits. Four sentences. The easier it is to say yes without a meeting, the more yeses you get.

Lead with something specific you actually noticed about their work — not flattery, evidence that you looked. Then make the ask small. A single collab post is a much easier first commitment than a three-month programme, and it's the audition for everything after it.

Expect silence more often than refusal. Send three pitches, not one.

## Agree the boring details first

This is where partnerships get awkward, and it's entirely preventable. Before anyone opens a design tool, write down:

- **The dates.** Both posts go live when? Same day or staggered?
- **Who makes what.** Who writes the caption, who shoots the photo, who gets final say on the image.
- **What's off-limits.** Claims neither brand will make, competitors neither will mention, discounts neither will undercut.
- **The links.** If anything is meant to drive traffic, both sides use [tracked links](/blog/how-to-tell-if-social-media-drives-sales) agreed in advance. Retrofitting attribution afterwards is impossible.
- **Who approves.** Two brands means two approval chains. Decide now who signs off on each side, or you'll discover it at 9pm the night before.

None of this needs a contract. It needs to exist in writing somewhere both of you can see.

## Run it on one calendar, not two

The most common failure in a brand partnership isn't a bad idea — it's a missed date. One side posts on Tuesday as agreed. The other side's post slips to Friday because something came up. The whole point of simultaneous reach evaporates.

Treat the partnership as a single campaign with entries on your [content calendar](/product/content-calendar) rather than a favour sitting in your inbox: the asset deadline, the approval window, both go-live slots, and the follow-up post. Schedule your side the moment the creative is approved so a busy week can't quietly eat it. If you're coordinating across two teams and two time zones, agree the publish time in one named time zone and let the tool do the conversion — "Tuesday morning" means two different things to two different cities.

The [approval step](/blog/content-approval-workflow-small-teams) matters more here than in your own content. A post that goes out under someone else's handle with a typo or an off-brand claim is a harder conversation than fixing your own mistake.

## How to tell whether it worked

Not the morning after. The morning after tells you about reach, which was never the goal.

Check three things a month later: how many of the new followers are still there and still engaging, how many clicks the tracked link actually produced, and whether anyone bought. Then add the question no dashboard answers — would you work with them again? A partnership that produced modest numbers and an easy, reliable counterpart is worth more than a spike with someone you'd rather not email twice.

The best outcome of a first partnership is a second one. Three or four standing relationships, cycled through the year, quietly solve the "how do we reach new people" problem that ads only rent you an answer to.

## Start with one

Pick the brand you already buy from. Pick the collab post. Pick a date three weeks out. Send four sentences.

That's the entire project — and it's a meaningfully different lever from [paying a creator](/blog/when-to-bring-in-a-creator-for-your-content-calendar), because when it works you haven't bought reach, you've built a relationship that keeps paying.
