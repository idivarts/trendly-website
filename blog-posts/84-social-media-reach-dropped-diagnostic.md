# Your Reach Dropped. Here's How to Diagnose It Before You Panic

You open the app on a Monday and the number is wrong. Last month's posts were reaching a few thousand accounts. This month they're reaching a few hundred. Nothing obvious changed. The first thought is always the same one: *I've been penalized.*

That thought is expensive. It leads people to delete posts, change format overnight, double their posting rate, or quit a channel that was working fine three weeks ago — and all of those destroy the evidence you'd need to work out what actually happened.

A drop in reach is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Here's the order to check things in, cheapest and most likely first.

## Step one: make sure you're comparing like with like

Before anything else, confirm the drop is real. A surprising share of reach "crashes" are artifacts of the comparison.

Check the obvious traps:

- **Different window lengths.** A 7-day view next to a 30-day view is not a comparison. Neither is a partial week against a full one.
- **Recent posts aren't finished yet.** Reach accumulates. Yesterday's post will always look weak next to one from three weeks ago, especially where [search and saves keep delivering views for months](/blog/social-seo-get-found-in-app-search/).
- **One outlier is skewing the baseline.** If a single post last month did five times your normal numbers, your "average" was never your average. Compare medians, or just read the list of individual posts.
- **You posted less.** Total reach falling because you published six times instead of twelve is a volume problem, not a reach problem, and it has a different fix.

Look at per-post reach for your last ten posts next to the ten before them. If the typical post is still landing in the same range, nothing is broken.

## Step two: separate reach from engagement rate

These are two different failures with two different causes, and conflating them sends you down the wrong path.

**Reach fell, engagement rate held steady.** Fewer people saw it; the ones who did responded normally. That points to distribution — posting volume, timing, format mix, or a platform-level change. The content itself is still working.

**Reach held, engagement rate fell.** The platform showed your post to the usual number of people and they scrolled past. That's a content problem: the hook, the topic, the format, or a drift toward posts that serve you more than the reader.

**Both fell together.** Normally the second caused the first: weaker engagement on recent posts meant less distribution on the next ones. Fix the content, not the schedule.

If you're unsure which numbers to trust here, our guide to [metrics that actually move the needle](/blog/social-metrics-that-actually-move-the-needle/) covers which figures are worth reading at all.

## Step three: check your own changelog first

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that explains most drops. Ask what changed on *your* side in the four to six weeks before the decline. Be honest and specific:

1. **Did your posting rhythm change?** A holiday gap, a launch week where you posted daily, a fortnight of silence. Both directions matter — a sudden spike in volume dilutes performance per post just as a gap cools distribution.
2. **Did your format mix change?** Platforms weight formats differently, so drifting from video to static images, or from carousels to single posts, moves reach on its own. Check our [format guide](/blog/choosing-the-right-social-content-format/) if you've shifted without noticing.
3. **Did your topic mix change?** Count the last fifteen posts and sort them into promotional and non-promotional. Many accounts slide into promotion during a launch and never slide back — see [finding your content mix](/blog/social-media-content-mix-how-much-to-promote/).
4. **Did someone else start posting?** A new freelancer, a co-founder, a batch written in a hurry. Voice shifts show up in engagement before anyone notices them consciously.
5. **Did you start pushing people off-platform?** A run of link-heavy posts after months of native content usually costs you reach.

Most of the time you'll find your answer in this list. Write it down before you look at anything else, because the next two steps are far more tempting and far less useful.

## Step four: check whether it's the platform, not you

Platform-wide shifts are real. Feeds get rebalanced, a new format gets promoted, recommendation of non-followed content gets dialed up or down. When it happens, it happens to everyone in your category at once.

The test is simple: look at five or six comparable accounts you already track. If their recent posts are also getting noticeably less visible engagement than their older ones, you're seeing a platform change, not a personal one. If they're fine and you're not, go back to step three.

Treat a confirmed platform shift as information about where to invest next, not as something to fight. If short video is being pushed and your reach came from static images, that's a planning decision for next month — not a reason to panic today.

## The shadowban question

Here's the uncomfortable part: "shadowban" is used to describe two very different things.

Platforms do restrict reach for specific, documented reasons — content that breaks a policy, content flagged as unoriginal or reposted, or accounts that trip spam heuristics through bulk following, repetitive comments, or engagement-pod behavior. Several platforms now surface this directly in account settings, with a status page telling you whether your account or a given post is eligible for recommendation. Check that page before theorizing; it's the only authoritative answer available to you.

What doesn't exist is the folk version: an invisible punishment applied at random, or for using too many hashtags, or for editing a caption. If the status page says you're eligible and your posts still reach your followers normally, you are not shadowbanned. You have a content or distribution problem — much better news, because you can fix it.

## What to actually do about it

Resist the urge to change five things at once. If you do, you'll never know which one worked, and you'll have burned the only clean baseline you had.

Pick the single most likely cause from your changelog, correct just that, and hold everything else steady for two to three weeks. Keep publishing at your normal [cadence](/blog/consistent-posting-cadence-without-burnout/) while you do — going quiet while you "figure it out" adds a second variable on top of the first.

Then re-run your best-performing formats deliberately. Not a strategy reinvention: just [bring back what already worked](/blog/recycle-best-performing-content/) and see whether it performs the way it used to. If an old reliable format still lands, your fundamentals are intact and the drop was situational. If it doesn't, the audience or the platform has moved — a strategy conversation, not an emergency.

Be honest about noise, too. When a typical post reaches a few hundred people, a sizeable swing between months can be ordinary variance. Our guide to [reading results at small scale](/blog/test-social-content-small-audience/) covers how long to wait before a difference means anything.

## Build the baseline before you need it

The reason this diagnostic is hard is almost always missing history. Most people can't remember what they posted six weeks ago, in what format, at what rate — so they can't answer step three, and they jump straight to blaming the algorithm.

The fix is unglamorous: keep the plan and the results in the same place. When your calendar, your drafts and your performance history live in one workspace instead of a spreadsheet, three apps and a notes file, the changelog writes itself. You scroll back, see that you went from four posts a week to one during a launch, and settle it in ten minutes instead of a month. A reach drop is rarely a verdict. It's usually just a question you can't answer yet.
