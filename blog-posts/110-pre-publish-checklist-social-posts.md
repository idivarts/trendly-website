# Don't Hit Publish Yet: A Pre-Publish Checklist for Social Posts

Nobody ever regrets the post they thought about for one extra minute.

The mistakes that sting on social media are almost never strategic. The strategy was fine. The pillar was right, the idea was good, the caption was decent. What went wrong was mechanical: the link went to a 404, the top of the image got cropped into the profile picture, the caption said "Thursday" and it published on Wednesday, the discount code had a typo, the post went out from the wrong account.

Those mistakes all happen in the same place — the last two minutes, when the work is basically done and you just want it off your desk. That's exactly when nobody is paying attention.

So build a check into that moment. Not a bureaucratic review, not a second opinion. A fast, boring list you run before anything leaves the drafts.

## Why re-reading it one more time doesn't work

Because you can't proofread your own caption. You wrote it; your brain supplies what you meant rather than what's on the screen. You'll read past the missing word four times in a row.

A checklist works differently. It doesn't ask "is this good?" — a question you've already answered and will answer the same way again. It asks specific, closed questions with yes/no answers: *does this link open?* *is there text inside the top 10% of the image?* *is the date right?* Those you can actually verify.

It's the same reason pilots run checklists for things they've done a thousand times. Familiar work is where attention goes first.

## The checklist

Five groups. Most posts clear the whole thing in under two minutes.

### 1. The copy

- **Read the first line alone.** On every feed, the first line is the whole post until someone taps "more". If it only makes sense as part of the paragraph, rewrite it.
- **Check the one fact in the post.** Price, date, time, spelling of a name, the city, the discount code. If the post contains a number, verify the number.
- **Say the first sentence out loud.** Typos survive silent reading and die instantly when spoken.
- **Confirm the ask.** There should be exactly one thing you want the reader to do, and it should be visible without expanding the caption. If you're not sure what you're asking for, that's worth [deciding deliberately](/blog/social-media-call-to-action-what-to-ask-for/) before this post ships.

### 2. The link

- **Click it.** In an incognito window, where you aren't logged in as an admin. A link that works for you and nobody else is a common and expensive failure.
- **Check where it actually lands.** Right page, not just the right domain. Sale page, not homepage.
- **Confirm it's in a place the platform allows.** Some surfaces strip links from captions, some only honour the profile link, some make link stickers a Story-only feature. If the link is the point of the post, know where it's going to live before you write the caption around it.
- **Check tracking.** If you're measuring whether social [drives sales](/blog/how-to-tell-if-social-media-drives-sales/), the UTM or tracked link has to be on the version that ships, not just the one in your notes.

### 3. The visual

- **Preview the real crop, per platform.** A square crop, a 4:5 feed crop, and a 9:16 full-screen crop cut the same image in three different places. Text near an edge is the first casualty.
- **Avoid the safe-zone traps.** The top of a vertical video sits under the account name; the bottom sits under the caption and buttons. Keep anything you need read out of both.
- **Check it at thumbnail size.** Shrink it on your screen. If the headline is unreadable at that size, it's unreadable in a feed.
- **Watch video with sound off, then on.** Most people see it muted first. Then check the audio actually exists and isn't clipped at the start.
- **Write the alt text and captions now.** Not later — later never arrives. There's a fuller list of what to include in our guide to [accessible social posts](/blog/social-media-accessibility-alt-text-captions/).

### 4. The platform-specific bits

- **Right account.** Brand page, not your personal profile. Client A's workspace, not client B's.
- **Tags and mentions.** Spelled right, pointing at the live handle, and worth having — a mention that doesn't resolve looks careless.
- **Hashtags where that platform wants them**, in the count that platform rewards. The number genuinely differs per network.
- **First comment.** If your links or hashtags live there, make sure it's queued, not something you intend to paste manually three minutes after publishing.
- **Caption length.** Check where each platform truncates. A caption that works on one network can get cut mid-sentence on another — which is the whole argument for adapting one message per channel rather than pasting it everywhere.

### 5. The schedule itself

- **Date and day of the week match the copy.** "Happy Friday" on a Thursday is the single most common scheduling error there is.
- **Time zone.** Confirm whether you're scheduling in your time zone or your audience's. For [distributed teams](/blog/one-calendar-every-time-zone-global-remote-teams/), agree on one convention and write it down.
- **Nothing collides.** Two posts twenty minutes apart on the same channel compete with each other.
- **Nothing is now inappropriate.** Scan the rest of the queue for anything that reads badly next to this post, or next to the news.

## Run it per batch, not per post

If you draft a month in one sitting, running a five-group check thirty separate times is how checklists die.

Batch it instead. Draft everything first. Then do one pass for copy and facts across all of it, one pass clicking every link, one pass previewing every visual, one pass over dates and times on the calendar. Same checks, a quarter of the context-switching — and you'll catch the cross-post problems that only show up when you look at the whole month at once, like three posts in a row making the same ask.

## When more than one person is involved

The checklist is not the review. Those are two different jobs, and conflating them is how both get skipped.

A reviewer answers "should we say this?" The pre-publish check answers "does this work?" Reviewers are bad at the second question — they read for message and skim the mechanics, assuming someone else handled the link.

So name an owner. Usually the person who schedules it runs the mechanical check, after approval, as the last action before the post enters the queue. If you have an [approval step](/blog/content-approval-workflow-small-teams/) already, put the checklist at the end of it, not inside it.

## Keep it where the work happens

A checklist on a sticky note is a checklist you'll stop using in a fortnight. Put it where the posts are: as a saved note in your workspace, a checklist on the draft, or a short step in your publishing routine that has to be ticked before anything moves to scheduled.

This is one of the quieter arguments for drafting, reviewing, and scheduling in the same place rather than across a doc, a chat thread, and a scheduler. When the brief, the draft, the approval, and the queue all sit on one [content calendar](/product/content-calendar/), the last check is a glance down a single screen instead of a reconstruction from four tabs — and it's obvious when a post is sitting in the queue without having had one.

Two minutes per post. Possibly the highest-return two minutes in your whole content process, precisely because it's the least interesting.
