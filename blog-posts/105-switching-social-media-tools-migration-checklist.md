# Switching Social Media Tools Without Losing a Week: A Migration Checklist

Ask a founder why they're still using a social tool they complain about every month and you'll rarely hear "it's good." You'll hear some version of: *moving everything would take a week I don't have.*

That instinct isn't irrational. A migration done badly means a gap in your feed, a disconnected account nobody notices for nine days, and a half-rebuilt calendar that makes the new tool look worse than the old one. Done in a deliberate order, it takes an afternoon of setup and a two-week overlap — and you keep posting the entire time.

## First, write down what you're actually replacing

Before you export anything, list every tool that touches one social post today. For most small teams the honest list is longer than expected: a scheduler, a doc where captions get drafted, a separate AI subscription (or three), a design tool, a folder of assets somewhere, and a group chat where approvals happen.

That list is your real scope. Compare *scheduler to scheduler* and you'll pick on the wrong criteria and keep the same sprawl. If three of those six tools collapse into the new one, the switch pays for itself in a way a feature-by-feature comparison never shows. We broke this down in [How Many Tools Does It Take to Publish One Post?](/blog/how-many-tools-to-publish-one-post-tool-stack-cost/) — the cost is mostly in the handoffs, not the subscriptions.

Write the list. Mark which items the new tool absorbs, which stay, and which you're keeping only out of habit.

## Pick the cut-over date before you start the trial

The most common migration failure is open-ended evaluation: a trial starts, nobody owns it, the old tool keeps running, and six weeks later you're paying for both and committed to neither.

Instead, set two dates up front:

- **Setup day** — one block of 2–3 hours to connect accounts and rebuild the calendar.
- **Cut-over date** — usually 10–14 days later, when the old tool stops being the source of truth.

Everything between those dates is the overlap window, and the overlap is the whole trick: your old tool is still publishing while you prove the new one works.

## Export the five things you can't rebuild from memory

You don't need to migrate your archive. Published posts already live on the platforms; nobody has ever needed a CSV of last March's tweets. What you genuinely can't reconstruct is the working material:

1. **Scheduled posts that haven't gone out yet.** This is the only time-critical item. Screenshot or export the queue — date, time, platform, caption, asset.
2. **Your evergreen captions and hooks.** The lines that reliably work, the CTA variants, the post formats you reuse.
3. **Your asset library.** Logos, product shots, brand fonts, the b-roll you keep reaching for. If these live in five places, this is the moment to consolidate them into one.
4. **Your brand voice notes.** Tone rules, words you never use, the way you write about pricing. If this only exists in someone's head, write it down now — any AI-assisted tool will ask for it, and it's what keeps generated drafts from sounding generic.
5. **Your recurring calendar structure.** The content pillars and standing slots, not the individual posts.

Most tools let you export some of this. For the rest, a single spreadsheet is fine — it's a transit document, not a permanent home.

## Reconnect accounts in the right order

This is where migrations quietly break, because social platforms treat app connections as permissions granted by a *person*, not a company.

Sequence it like this:

1. **Confirm who holds admin on each platform account** before you touch anything. If your Instagram is connected through a personal Facebook profile belonging to a contractor who left, you'll find out during the migration — and that's a separate, longer problem. Our [account ownership checklist](/blog/who-owns-your-social-accounts-access-checklist/) covers how to fix it properly.
2. **Connect the new tool with the right account** — the business-owner login, not whoever happens to be at the desk.
3. **Verify each connection by publishing one real post** per platform. Not a test post to a dummy account. A real, small, scheduled post, so you see the whole path work end to end.
4. **Leave the old tool connected** until after cut-over. Disconnecting early is the one action that can actually cause a missed post.

Do not disconnect anything on setup day. Connections are additive; both tools can hold a valid connection at once.

## Rebuild forward, not backward

On setup day, load only the next two weeks into the new tool. That's enough to exercise every part of the workflow — drafting, generating, reviewing, scheduling, publishing, replying — without the sunk-cost feeling of having rebuilt a quarter inside a tool you might abandon. If it survives two weeks of real use, extend to the full month. If it doesn't, you've lost an afternoon instead of a week.

During the overlap, follow one rule: **every new post gets created in the new tool only.** Anything already queued in the old tool stays there and ships from there. No double-entry, no "I'll keep both in sync." Double-entry is how people conclude the new tool is more work.

## Move the team, not just the content

If anyone else touches your social — a co-founder who approves, a part-time designer, a contractor writing captions — their habits are part of the migration.

Three things to do before cut-over:

- **Invite them on setup day,** not after. A tool nobody else has opened becomes your tool, and the approvals drift back into chat.
- **Run one real approval through the new workflow.** Draft → review → request a change → approve → schedule. Once, with a post that's actually going out.
- **Agree where feedback lives now.** The reason approvals end up scattered across chat threads is that nobody declared the new place. Declare it. If you want a structure for this, see [From Draft to Published](/blog/content-approval-workflow-small-teams/).

## Cut over, then clean up

On your cut-over date, let the old tool finish its remaining queue, then stop creating anything in it. Give yourself one more week — enough to notice anything you forgot — then cancel properly, revoke its platform connections, and remove the seats. Cancelling is the step people skip for months, so put it on the calendar on cut-over day.

## The short version

- List every tool in the path of one post, not just the scheduler.
- Set a setup day and a cut-over date before you start.
- Export the queue, the evergreen copy, the assets, the voice notes, the calendar structure. Skip the archive.
- Check platform admin rights first, connect fresh, verify with one real post each, disconnect nothing early.
- Load two weeks forward. Create new work only in the new tool.
- Bring the team in on day one and run one real approval.
- Cut over, wait a week, then cancel and revoke.
