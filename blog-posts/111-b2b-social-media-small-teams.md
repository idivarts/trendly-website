# Your Buyer Is a Committee: B2B Social Media for Small Teams

The B2B version of the social media complaint sounds like this: "We post twice a week, we get four likes, and none of it turns into anything."

Then a demo request comes in, and on the call the prospect says *"I've been reading your LinkedIn posts for a few months."* They never liked a post, never commented, never followed. The entire relationship happened somewhere your analytics tab can't see.

That gap — between what B2B social does and what B2B social *shows* — is why so many teams quit right before it starts working. The channel isn't broken. The feedback loop is.

## Your buyer isn't one person, and isn't ready

Two facts change everything about how you plan B2B content.

**First: the decision isn't made by the person you're talking to.** In most B2B purchases the person who finds you is not the person who signs. There's someone who will own the tool, someone who worries about cost, someone who worries about security or compliance, and someone who has to be convinced this isn't another thing to maintain. Content that only speaks to the champion dies in the internal conversation you never attend.

**Second: almost nobody in your audience is buying this week.** At any given moment, the overwhelming majority of companies that could buy from you are not in-market. They have a contract that renews in eight months, a bigger fire, or no awareness that the problem is solvable. You cannot convert them. You can only be the name they already recognize when the fire starts.

So B2B social has a different job description from the one most calendars are built around. It is not a conversion channel. It is a **familiarity and objection-handling channel** that conversion flows through later.

## The three jobs B2B content actually does

Score every post idea against these. If it does none of them, cut it.

1. **Make you familiar.** Recognition is a shortcut buyers use when they're finally shopping. The vendor they've seen explain the problem intelligently for six months starts the evaluation already shortlisted.
2. **Answer the objection that stalls deals.** Every sales conversation has the same three or four sticking points. "How long does migration take?" "What happens to our existing data?" "Do we need a specialist to run it?" Every one of those is a post, and answering it publicly means it's handled before the call instead of during it.
3. **Arm your internal champion.** Someone inside a prospect company has to sell you to their boss. A clear post, a short demo video, a one-screen comparison — those get forwarded into Slack threads you'll never see. Make things that are easy to forward.

Notice what's missing: booth announcements, milestone self-congratulation, quote graphics. Not banned — but a feed made only of those does none of the three jobs.

## What to post when nobody buys from a post

Four categories will carry a B2B feed almost indefinitely.

**The work.** How you actually do the thing: a before/after of a messy process, the checklist you run internally, a decision and why you made it that way. The most under-used B2B content type, because it feels unremarkable from the inside — and the most credible thing you own.

**The point of view.** What you believe about your category that competitors won't say out loud. This is what creates recognition. It also filters: the people who disagree were never going to buy, and the people who nod are pre-sold.

**The honest proof.** Not fabricated numbers — the real material. A customer problem described without naming the customer. A screenshot of the actual product. What your approach *doesn't* do well, and who should buy something else. Admitting a limit buys more trust in B2B than any superlative.

**The useful thing.** A template, a checklist, a teardown, a plain-English explainer of something confusing in your industry. This is what gets saved and forwarded, which is the real distribution mechanism in B2B — not the like button. ## Be in fewer places, properly

B2B teams spread themselves across five platforms and do all of them badly. Pick based on where your buyer already is:

- **LinkedIn** is the default for most B2B. It's the one place being at work is the whole context.
- **X** still matters if you sell to developers, founders, or technical operators — much less otherwise.
- **YouTube** earns its slot for long evaluation cycles. Buyers watch a 12-minute walkthrough before a demo call, and that video keeps working for years.
- **Instagram and TikTok** make sense when your buyer is a small-business owner rather than an enterprise function. Plenty of B2B is like that. Most isn't.

Two platforms done consistently beat five done occasionally. Our [platform decision framework](/blog/which-social-platforms-should-your-brand-be-on/) walks through the choice in detail.

One more thing worth planning deliberately: in B2B, personal accounts almost always out-reach the company page. That's not a reason to abandon the page — it's a reason to run both on purpose, which we covered in [founder-led content](/blog/founder-led-content-personal-brand-vs-company-page/).

## Measuring it without clean attribution

B2B attribution is genuinely bad, and pretending otherwise leads teams to kill working channels. Use a stack of imperfect signals instead of one fake-precise number:

- **Ask on the form.** A "How did you hear about us?" free-text field on your demo request teaches you more than any dashboard. Self-reported attribution is noisy but directionally honest.
- **Watch branded search.** If people start searching your company name directly, something upstream is working.
- **Log it in the CRM.** Have sales note when a prospect mentions a post, a video, or a founder's account. Three mentions a month is a signal.
- **Track the leading indicators, not the likes.** Profile visits, saves, newsletter signups, inbound DMs, and reply quality move before pipeline does.
- **Judge follower quality, not count.** Two hundred followers with the right job titles is a better asset than ten thousand of nobody in particular.

## A cadence two people can actually hold

Three posts a week, assigned by job, is enough:

- **One point of view** — something you believe, argued in under 200 words.
- **One piece of proof or process** — the work, a screenshot, a real problem solved.
- **One useful thing** — the explainer, template, or teardown people save.

Add twenty minutes of replying to other people's posts, because in B2B the comment section is where the DM starts. Everything else — events, hiring, milestones — slots in around that skeleton rather than replacing it.

## Start with the objections

Don't start with a content calendar. Open your last ten sales conversations and write down every question that slowed a deal down. That list is your first month of B2B social content, it does job two immediately, and it's the one input no competitor can copy from you.
