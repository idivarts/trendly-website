# The DM Is the Sales Call: How to Handle Inbound Messages Without Losing the Sale

Most small brands put enormous effort into the post and almost none into what happens after it works.

Someone watches your reel, taps through to your profile, and sends four words: "How much is it?" That message is the warmest moment you will get all week. And it usually gets answered eleven hours later, with a number and nothing else, by a founder typing with one thumb between other jobs.

Content gets the conversation started. The DM is where it's won or lost. Here's a system for that part — no scripts that sound like a bot, no CRM to buy.

## Treat the DM as the last mile, not an interruption

If you've already built a posting rhythm, you've built an engine that produces intent: people who now know you exist and are curious enough to reach out. That intent has a shelf life measured in hours, not days.

The mistake is filing DMs under "admin" alongside spam follows and partnership pitches. They're not admin. A reply to a pricing question is worth more than the next three posts you publish, because the person has already done every hard step except asking.

So the first decision is a calendar one: your content plan needs a slot for replying, not just for publishing. Fifteen minutes in the morning, fifteen after lunch. That's usually enough — if the time exists at all.

## Sort every message in five seconds

Not all DMs are sales conversations, and treating them identically is what makes the inbox feel endless. Before you type anything, drop the message into one of four buckets:

- **Buyer** — asking about price, availability, shipping, sizing, fit, timelines. Reply first, reply today.
- **Curious** — complimenting the post, asking a general question, not close to buying. Reply warmly, briefly, no pitch.
- **Noise** — pitches, growth-hack spam, unsolicited offers. Archive without guilt.
- **Problem** — an order gone wrong, a complaint moved out of public view. Different playbook entirely; see our [guide to handling negative comments](/blog/how-to-handle-negative-comments-social-media/).

Only the first bucket deserves a real conversation. Sorting first means you spend your energy on the five messages that matter instead of the fifty that don't.

## Answer the price question — then ask one of your own

The instinct when someone asks "how much?" is either to dodge ("let's jump on a call!") or to drop a bare number and wait. Dodging reads as evasive. A bare number invites silent comparison shopping.

Do both halves instead: answer plainly, then ask one question that moves things forward.

> "It's $180, and that includes the fitting. Are you looking for something for a particular date, or just browsing options for now?"

That single question does three jobs. It tells you whether there's a deadline, which is the strongest buying signal there is. It gives you something specific to follow up on later. And it keeps the thread alive.

One question — not a qualification form. The fastest way to kill a warm DM is to make the person feel processed.

## Write the twelve replies you send every week

Here's the uncomfortable truth about inbound messages: there are maybe a dozen distinct ones. Price. Shipping time. Do you have it in another size. Do you work with businesses like mine. Can I see examples. What's the turnaround. Do you ship internationally.

You already answer these constantly — just inconsistently, depending on how tired you are. So write them once, properly, while you're thinking clearly. Then keep them somewhere you can reach in two seconds.

A good saved reply is short, specific, and ends with a question or a next step. It is not a paragraph of brand voice. And it is never pasted in raw — change the first line so it responds to what the person actually said. The template saves you the structure, not the thinking.

This is also the single highest-leverage thing to do before you hand the inbox to anyone else. A library of good replies is how a part-time helper answers like you do. It's the same principle as [handing off social without losing your voice](/blog/delegate-social-media-without-losing-brand-voice/): write the standard down, then delegate the standard.

## Follow up once, specifically, and then stop

Most inbound conversations die in the gap. Someone asks, you answer, they say "amazing, thanks!" — and nothing happens. They got distracted, not uninterested.

One follow-up, two or three days later, recovers a meaningful share of those. The rule: reference something specific from the conversation, and make it easy to say no.

> "Hey — you mentioned you needed it before the 14th. Still want me to hold one? Totally fine if plans changed."

That's it. One nudge, then let it go. Chasing a third time costs you more in reputation than the sale is worth, and people who want to come back usually do.

Keep the list of open threads somewhere visible — a note, a board column, a task per conversation. Memory is not a system.

## Automate the predictable part, keep the human part human

Some of this genuinely should be automatic. When one post drives two hundred people to ask for the same link, nobody should be typing it two hundred times. That's what comment-and-DM automation is for, and it's the whole idea behind [Impulse](/product/impulse/) — a comment triggers the link or the next step instantly, while the person is still interested.

The line worth holding: automate the **delivery of known information** — a link, a price list, a size chart, a booking page. Keep the **conversation** human, especially anything involving money, a deadline, or a disappointed customer.

Automation buys back the volume. It shouldn't buy back the relationship. We wrote more on where that line sits in our [intro to comment-to-cart automation](/blog/comment-to-cart-automation-turning-comments-into-customers/).

## Let the inbox write your next month of content

Every DM is unfiltered market research, and it's free. The questions people ask you privately are the exact objections sitting between a stranger and a purchase — phrased in their words, not your marketing's.

Keep a running tally for a month. The question that comes up seven times is a post. The confusion that comes up constantly is a pinned story, a carousel, or a line in your bio that shouldn't have been missing in the first place. If three people a week ask whether you ship to Canada, that's not an FAQ problem, it's a content problem.

This is the loop that compounds: content creates conversations, conversations reveal what to make next, and the next round of content pre-answers the questions — so the DMs that arrive are closer to "I'll take it" than "how much?"

## Where this fits in the bigger system

A brand that posts consistently and ignores its inbox is leaving money in a notification badge. The fix isn't working harder on either half — it's treating them as one job: publish, then reply, on the same calendar, in the same place.

That's the thinking behind building the planner, the calendar, and the [unified inbox](/blog/unified-inbox-social-comments-dms-workflow/) into one workspace instead of three — so you're not switching apps to find out whether Monday's post turned into anything by Friday.

Start with the two fifteen-minute slots and the twelve saved replies. You'll feel the difference inside a week — and so will the person who asked how much it costs.
