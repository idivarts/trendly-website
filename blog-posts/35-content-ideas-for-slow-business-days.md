# What to Post When Nothing's Happening: Content Ideas for Slow Business Days

Every founder hits the same wall. You've shipped the launch, the campaign is over, the big customer win was three weeks ago — and now the calendar is empty and there's genuinely nothing "newsworthy" to say. So you skip a day. Then a week. Then your audience forgets you exist.

Here's the reframe that fixes it: **most great social content isn't news.** It's not tied to a product drop or a milestone. The brands that stay top-of-mind post consistently through the quiet stretches, because they've learned that a slow business week is a content problem, not a content drought.

Below is a working list of ideas for exactly those days — plus a system so you stop having this problem at all.

## First, drop the "it has to be an announcement" rule

The instinct to only post when something happens is what makes social feel exhausting. If every post has to be an event, you'll post rarely and awkwardly.

The truth is that your audience doesn't follow you for press releases. They follow you because your topic is useful, interesting, or entertaining to them. That's an infinite well. You already know more about your niche than 95% of the people scrolling — a slow day is just a chance to share some of it.

So when nothing's happening, don't reach for news. Reach for one of these.

## 1. Answer a question you get all the time

The single most reliable content source you own is your inbox. Every question a customer asks is a post. "How long does shipping take?" "Can I use this if I'm a beginner?" "What's the difference between X and Y?"

Write the answer once, publicly. If three people asked it, three hundred were wondering. This is evergreen, genuinely helpful, and it quietly does sales work for you.

## 2. Show how something is made or done

Process content performs far above its effort level. Film 20 seconds of you packing an order, sketching an idea, testing a prototype, or setting up for the day. It doesn't need to be polished — it needs to be real.

People are endlessly curious about how the sausage gets made. A slow day at the desk is the *perfect* time to capture it, precisely because nothing dramatic is happening.

## 3. Share an opinion your industry argues about

Pick something you actually believe and say it plainly. "Discounting trains customers to wait." "You don't need a huge following to make money." "Most productivity advice is procrastination in disguise."

A clear point of view is magnetic — it gives people something to agree with, push back on, or tag a friend about. Just make sure it's a real opinion, not manufactured outrage.

## 4. Teach one small, specific thing

Not a masterclass. One tip. The tighter the better: "The one setting I change on every new phone." "How I write a subject line in 30 seconds." Small, concrete, immediately usable.

These are easy to make and easy to save, and saves are the signal most algorithms quietly reward.

## 5. Repurpose your best-ever post

Look back through your feed and find the post that outperformed everything else. Then make it again — different angle, different format, updated example. Your audience has largely turned over since then, and even the people who saw it won't remember. Your greatest hits are a renewable resource.

## 6. Tell a short story about a customer

No name-drop or testimonial claim required. "Someone messaged us last week trying to solve [problem]. Here's what we told them." A specific, human moment beats a generic benefit statement every time.

## 7. Give a behind-the-numbers or lessons-learned post

Share something you figured out the hard way. A mistake you made, an assumption that turned out wrong, a workflow you finally fixed. Founders who are honest about the messy middle build far more trust than the ones who only post wins.

## 8. React to something happening in your world

You don't need your *own* news — you can borrow the moment. A new tool everyone's talking about, a trend in your industry, a seasonal shift. Add your take. This keeps you relevant without needing anything to happen in your own business.

## Turn "slow-day ideas" into a permanent bank

Notice the pattern: none of these ideas required news. They required a *prompt* — a category to pull from. The reason slow days feel hard is that you're trying to invent content from a blank page under pressure. The fix is to never start from blank.

Keep a running list of the categories above and, underneath each, drop half-formed ideas whenever they hit you — a customer question, a strong opinion, a lesson learned. This is your [idea bank](/blog/build-an-idea-bank-for-social-content), and once it exists, "what do I post today?" becomes "which of these 40 ideas fits today?"

Pair it with a small set of [content pillars](/blog/content-pillars-picking-themes-your-brand-posts-about) — the three to five themes you always come back to — and slow days basically disappear. You're never generating from scratch; you're selecting.

## A simple slow-day routine

When you're stuck, run this in order:

1. **Check your inbox** for a question worth answering publicly.
2. **Skim your idea bank** for anything half-written you can finish.
3. **Look at your top post** from the last few months and remake it.
4. **Ask your AI workspace** for ten fresh angles tied to your pillars.
5. Pick one, make it in the next 20 minutes, and schedule it.

You'll almost never get past step two.

## The real lesson

Consistency isn't about always having something to announce — it's about having a system that produces something to *say*, even when nothing's happening. Slow business days are only a crisis if your content depends on your business making news.

Build the bank, lean on your pillars, and let AI handle the blank page. Then a quiet week becomes what it should be: just another week you showed up.
