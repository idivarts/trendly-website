You wrote a good post. The hook lands, the middle earns attention, the visual is clean. Then you get to the last line and type the thing everyone types: *"Link in bio!"* Or worse, nothing at all.

The call to action is the part of a post people spend the least time on and then blame for the lack of results. It deserves more thought than it usually gets — not because a clever CTA rescues a weak post, but because a mismatched one quietly wastes a good one.

Here's a practical way to decide what each post should actually ask for.

## The two failure modes

Almost every weak CTA falls into one of two buckets.

**Asking for nothing.** The post ends. No next step, no invitation, not even a question. These posts can still do work — awareness is real — but if every post ends this way, you've built an audience that has never once been asked to do anything. The first time you do ask, it feels abrupt.

**Asking for everything.** "Follow for more, save this, share it with a founder who needs it, comment YES, and grab the free guide in my bio." Five asks is zero asks. The reader's brain does what it does with any crowded choice: nothing.

The fix for both is the same discipline — **one ask per post**, chosen on purpose.

## Match the ask to where the reader actually is

A CTA fails most often because it asks for more commitment than the post earned. Someone who just watched a 12-second tip is not ready to book a call. Someone who read a detailed teardown of a problem they have might be.

Roughly, there are five levels of ask, from cheapest to most expensive:

1. **Attention asks** — save this, watch to the end, read the second slide. Nearly free for the reader.
2. **Response asks** — a genuine question, a "which of these two" poll, "tell me your version." Cheap, and the replies feed your next posts.
3. **Relationship asks** — follow for the rest of the series, turn on notifications, join the newsletter.
4. **Consideration asks** — read the full guide, watch the walkthrough, see how it works.
5. **Commercial asks** — start a trial, book a call, buy.

The rule of thumb: the CTA should be **one level above** the effort the reader just spent. A quick scroll-stopping post earns an attention or response ask. A post that taught something substantial can earn a consideration ask. Commercial asks belong on posts that have already done the convincing — a customer story, a before/after, a specific objection answered.

This is also why "how much should I promote?" is the wrong first question. Decide the *level* of each ask first; the promotional ratio sorts itself out. (We went deeper on that balance in [how much of your content should promote your product](/blog/social-media-content-mix-how-much-to-promote/).)

## Write the CTA as a sentence, not a label

"Link in bio" is a label. It tells someone where to click, not why they'd want to.

Compare:

- *"Link in bio."*
- *"The full 12-point checklist is in my bio — it's the same one we run before every launch."*

Second one does three things the first doesn't: it names what they get, it hints at the format (a checklist, not a 3,000-word essay), and it gives a reason to trust it. Same click, far more reason to make it.

A CTA sentence that works usually contains: **the action**, **the thing they get**, and **why now**. You don't need all three every time, but if you have none of them, you have a label.

## Respect the link friction on each platform

Every platform charges a different toll for sending people off it, and the CTA should bend to that reality rather than fight it.

- **Instagram** — no clickable links in feed captions. Your options are bio, stories, or asking people to comment or DM a keyword. Make the bio destination match whatever you're currently talking about rather than a generic homepage; see [profile and bio optimization](/blog/social-media-profile-bio-optimization/).
- **LinkedIn** — links work but reach is uneven. Many accounts put the link in the first comment and say so in the post. Test it on your own account before treating either approach as gospel.
- **X** — links are normal and expected. Keep the context above the link, not below it.
- **TikTok and Reels** — the CTA is usually spoken or on-screen, not in the caption. If it isn't said out loud in the last three seconds, most viewers never get it.
- **YouTube** — the description and pinned comment do real work here, and long-form earns heavier asks than any other format.

One caption, posted identically to five platforms, will have the wrong CTA on at least three of them. Adapting the ask per platform is the single highest-value edit you can make to a cross-posted piece.

## Not every CTA should drive a click

The most underrated asks don't send anyone anywhere.

**Saves** tell you a post has reference value — that's your signal to turn it into a series. **Comments** give you raw material: the questions underneath the replies are next month's posts. **DMs** are where small brands actually close business, which is why "DM me the word PLAN and I'll send it over" often outperforms a link — it starts a conversation instead of a bounce.

And some posts should simply end with a statement. A sharp observation that needs no button is a perfectly good way to finish.

## Plan CTAs across the month, not per post

Here's where most of the improvement actually comes from. When you write CTAs one post at a time, you repeat yourself without noticing — four newsletter pushes in a week, then nothing for a month.

Look at your calendar as a whole and plan the asks the way you plan the topics. A workable shape for a month:

- Most posts: attention or response asks
- A handful: relationship asks (newsletter, follow the series)
- A few: consideration asks
- One or two: direct commercial asks, placed on your strongest posts

Seen on a calendar, the gaps are obvious — a whole week where nothing invites a reply, or three signup pushes stacked on consecutive days. ## How to tell if a CTA is working

Judge a CTA against its own level, not against revenue. Response asks should produce replies. Relationship asks should produce subscribers. Only commercial asks should be judged on sales — and even then, attribution on social is genuinely hard, which we covered in [how to tell if social media drives sales](/blog/how-to-tell-if-social-media-drives-sales/).

Give any change four weeks before you draw conclusions, change one thing at a time, and remember that a CTA cannot fix a post nobody wanted to read. Fix the [post itself](/blog/anatomy-of-a-high-performing-social-post/) first; then make the ask worth the attention it earned.
