# Why Your AI-Written Posts Sound Like Everyone Else's (And the Five-Minute Fix)

Ask any AI model to write an Instagram caption about a new product launch and you'll get something like this in under two seconds: "Big news! We're so excited to finally share this with you 🎉 This has been a labor of love, and we can't wait for you to try it." It's grammatically perfect. It's also a caption that could belong to a skincare brand, a SaaS tool, or a taco truck. That's the problem — not that AI writes badly, but that it writes *safely*, and safe writing is what generic sounds like.

If you've felt this — pasting a draft into your calendar and thinking "this is fine, but it's not us" — you're not misusing the tool. You're seeing exactly what a language model is built to do: predict the most statistically likely next words. The most likely words, by definition, are the ones everyone uses. The fix isn't a better prompt alone. It's a short, repeatable edit pass that takes the median draft and makes it sound like one specific business wrote it.

## Why every AI draft starts out sounding the same

A model trained on millions of captions learns the *average* caption. Average is smooth, upbeat, and safe — which is exactly why it reads as hollow. There's no version of "we're so excited to announce" that carries your business's specific point of view, because that phrase was never written by your business. It was written by the statistical center of everyone's marketing copy.

This isn't a reason to skip AI drafts. A first draft in five seconds beats a blank page every time, and the strategy, structure, and timing an AI gets right saves real hours. The issue is treating the first draft as the final draft. The gap between "AI wrote this" and "this sounds like us" is almost always closed in the edit, not the prompt.

## The five tells of a generic AI draft

Before you can fix generic copy, you need to be able to spot it fast. Read your last AI-drafted post out loud and check for these:

**1. The hype opener.** "Exciting news," "we're thrilled to," "big announcement" — these tell the reader nothing about what's actually happening. Cut the opener and start with the fact.

**2. The vague benefit.** "This will change the way you [do the thing]" or "built to make your life easier" — benefit claims with no specific detail attached. If you can't name *what* changes, the reader can't either.

**3. The emoji sprinkle.** 🎉 ✨ 🙌 dropped in as punctuation rather than meaning. One well-placed emoji does work; three decorative ones read as filler.

**4. The hedge.** "We think you're going to love this" or "hopefully this helps" — soft language that a confident brand voice doesn't need. Say the thing directly.

**5. The missing specific.** No number, no name, no detail unique to your business — nothing a competitor's copy couldn't also claim. This is the biggest tell, and the easiest to fix.

If a caption has two or more of these, it's not ready to publish yet — but it's usually one short pass away.

## The edit pass: five swaps, five minutes

Once you can see the tells, fixing them is mechanical. Work through the draft with these five swaps:

**Swap the opener for the fact.** Instead of "Exciting news — our new dashboard is live," try "Our new dashboard is live: you can now see all three of your channels on one screen." Same information, but it leads with substance instead of enthusiasm.

**Replace the vague benefit with a real number or scenario.** "Saves you time" becomes "cuts your Monday planning session from 90 minutes to 20." If you don't have an exact number, describe a specific before/after moment instead of an abstract claim.

**Cut two of every three emoji.** Keep the one that adds meaning (a checkmark, an arrow), drop the ones that are just decoration.

**Delete the hedge word.** Scan for "we think," "hopefully," "might," "could" — and either cut them or replace them with a direct statement. Confidence reads as more trustworthy, not more arrogant.

**Add one detail only your business would say.** A customer's actual words, an internal number, a specific reason you made a decision, an opinion you're willing to defend. This single line is usually what turns a passable caption into a memorable one — it's the sentence a competitor literally could not publish, because it didn't happen to them.

That's the whole pass. It rarely takes more than five minutes once you know what you're looking for, and it's the difference between "on brand, technically" and "unmistakably ours."

## Build the edit into your workflow, not just your memory

Knowing the five swaps helps for the next caption you personally edit. It doesn't help if three people on your team are drafting with AI and only one of them knows to look for hedge words. That's a workflow gap, not a training gap — and it's exactly what a **generate → review → edit → approve** step is for.

In practice, this looks like a shared queue where every AI draft lands as a draft, not a scheduled post. A second set of eyes reads it against the five tells above, edits directly or requests an AI rewrite with a specific note ("make the opener a fact, not hype"), and only then approves it to publish. This is a genuinely different problem from writing a good prompt — it's making sure "does this sound like us" gets asked *every time*, by whoever is actually reviewing that day, not just by whoever happens to remember the checklist. Trendly builds this review step directly into the content calendar, so drafts, edits, and approvals all live in the same place the post gets scheduled from — nobody's copying text into a doc to get a second opinion.

## When "good enough" really is good enough

Not every post needs the full five-minute pass. A quick behind-the-scenes story, a reply to a trending moment, a low-stakes reminder post — these can go out closer to the raw AI draft, because the cost of being slightly generic is low and the cost of overthinking a throwaway post is real.

Save the edit pass for posts that are doing real work: launches, anything with your product name in it, anything meant to be shared or saved, and anything going out under your own name rather than a general brand account. Knowing which posts deserve five minutes and which don't is itself a useful skill — and one more reason a fast first draft from AI is a feature, not a shortcut you should feel guilty about.

## The takeaway

AI content sounds generic for a structural reason — it's trained to predict the average, and average has no point of view. That's not a flaw to route around; it's just what a first draft is for. The five tells (hype opener, vague benefit, emoji sprinkle, hedge, missing specific) tell you exactly what to fix, and the swap for each one is quick enough to do on every post that matters. Do it consistently, build it into how your team reviews drafts, and the AI keeps doing the heavy lifting — while the words that go out still sound like nobody but you.
