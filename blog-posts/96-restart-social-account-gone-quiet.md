# It's Been Four Months Since Your Last Post. Here's How to Come Back.

There's a particular kind of dread that comes with opening your own social account after a long silence. The last post is from a season that has already ended. The comments you never replied to are still sitting there. Somebody asked a question in March.

So you close the app again. Another week passes.

Here's the thing worth knowing before you do anything else: the gap is not the problem. The gap is a symptom. Something made posting unsustainable — a launch, a hire, a busy quarter, or a workflow that took three hours to produce one post. If you restart without fixing that, you'll be writing this same post to yourself in April.

This is the restart sequence: fix the foundation, decide what to say about the silence, then get two weeks live.

## Step 1: Audit before you post (15 minutes, not an afternoon)

A dormant account usually has small broken things that make a restart look worse than it is. Open your profile on a phone, as a stranger would, and check five things:

1. **The bio.** Does it still describe what you actually sell? Founders pivot and bios don't follow. If you need a longer fix, our [guide to profile bios](/blog/social-media-profile-bio-optimization) covers it properly.
2. **The link.** Click it. Dead links and expired campaign URLs are extremely common on quiet accounts.
3. **The pinned or featured posts.** A pinned post promoting a sale that ended two quarters ago is the single loudest signal that nobody's home. Unpin it.
4. **Unanswered comments and DMs.** Reply to anything from a real human, even late. "Sorry for the slow reply — are you still looking for this?" closes more business than people expect.
5. **Your last five posts.** Which one got the most saves, shares, or replies? You're going to reuse it in step 4.

That's the whole audit. If you want the longer version — the one that looks at every channel and decides which to keep — read [how to audit your social presence in an afternoon](/blog/audit-your-social-presence-in-an-afternoon).

## Step 2: Decide what to say about the gap

Founders agonise over this. The rule is simple: **one line, or nothing.**

Say nothing if your audience is mostly people who weren't paying close attention anyway — which, for most small brands, is nearly everyone. Nobody is keeping a ledger of your posting history. Start posting and no one will mention it.

Say one line if the silence was visible and explainable: "We went quiet while we rebuilt the product. Here's what changed." Honest, brief, and it gives people a reason to re-engage.

What you should not do is an apology tour. A post that's mostly about your relationship with posting is a post about you, not about the reader.

## Step 3: Pick a cadence you can hold for eight weeks — not your old one

The most common restart failure is reinstating an ambitious schedule out of guilt. You posted five times a week before you burned out, so you restart at five times a week — and three weeks later you're quiet again.

Instead, pick the number you could hit during your worst week of the quarter. For a lot of solo founders that's two. Two posts a week, held for eight weeks, is sixteen posts and a working habit. Five a week, held for nine days, is nine posts and a second abandonment. You can always add later: going from two to three feels like momentum, where going from five to two feels like failure, even though the output is identical.

If your old cadence collapsed under workload rather than ambition, [keeping a consistent posting cadence without burning out](/blog/consistent-posting-cadence-without-burnout) is the diagnosis to read next.

## Step 4: A two-week restart sequence

Don't open the app and freestyle. Draft all of these in one sitting — an hour, maybe ninety minutes — and schedule them. The point is to get out of the "will I post today?" decision entirely.

**Post 1 — the re-entry.** What you're doing now, in plain language. If you're acknowledging the gap, it's the one line here. Make the back half genuinely useful: a lesson, a change, a thing you learned while you were away.

**Post 2 — your proven piece, rebuilt.** Take the best-performing post from your audit and make it again, properly. New visual, tighter opening, updated detail. Most of your audience never saw it; the ones who did won't mind. (More on this in [recycling your best-performing content](/blog/recycle-best-performing-content).)

**Post 3 — answer a real question.** Pull one from your inbox, your sales calls, or your support email. Questions you've actually been asked outperform topics you invented, because somebody already cared enough to ask.

**Post 4 — show the work.** A process shot, a workspace, a half-finished thing, a decision you're weighing. This is the post that signals a human is back at the keyboard.

**Post 5 — ask for one specific thing.** Not "let us know your thoughts." A reply to a concrete question, a save, a click to one page. One ask, named clearly.

**Post 6 — useful and evergreen.** A how-to, a checklist, a short teardown. This is the post you'll still be able to reuse in six months.

Six posts. Two weeks at three a week, three weeks at two a week. Either way, you're live and the hardest part is behind you.

## About the algorithm

A quick reality check, because this is where restarts get psyched out: platforms don't keep a penalty box for inactivity. What's actually happening is colder and more fixable — your audience is out of the habit of seeing you, and engagement signals build on recent engagement signals. That recovers with consistency, and faster if you're present in other people's comments. A [20-minute engagement routine](/blog/daily-engagement-routine-commenting-to-grow) does more for a restart than another round of posting into silence.

So judge the first two weeks on whether you published, not on reach. Reach is a lagging indicator of a habit you don't have yet.

## Step 5: Make the restart survivable

Whatever broke the first time is still there unless you changed it. Usually it's one of three things:

- **The production cost per post was too high.** Writing, designing, and publishing one post across three platforms, in four tools, is a 40-minute job that feels like a two-hour job. Cut the step count before you cut the ambition.
- **There was no plan, so every day started from a blank page.** A month of planned themes is far less work than thirty improvised decisions.
- **Everything lived in your head.** If the plan isn't written down somewhere you'll actually look, it doesn't survive a busy week.

## The restart checklist

- Bio accurate, link working, stale pins removed
- Old comments and DMs replied to
- Best previous post identified for reuse
- Gap decision made: one line or nothing
- Cadence set at your worst-week number
- Six posts drafted and scheduled
- Next month's themes sketched, even roughly

Four months of silence is not a brand crisis. It's a few broken links, one awkward first post, and a workflow that needs to cost less than it used to.
