# Your Social Media Budget, Line by Line: What Small Brands Should Actually Pay For

"How much should we spend on social media?" is the question every founder asks, and it has no good answer. Any number someone gives you — a percentage of revenue, a dollar figure, a benchmark from a report — is a number pulled from a business that isn't yours.

The better question is **what should the money buy, and in what order?** Because a small brand's social budget isn't one number. It's four line items, each of which solves a different bottleneck. Spend on the wrong one and you get nothing — not because the amount was too small, but because you bought the wrong thing.

Here's the line-by-line version.

## First: you already have a budget, and it's measured in hours

Before any money moves, count the time. If you're a founder posting three times a week, you're probably spending four to six hours a week on social — writing, finding images, scheduling, replying to comments, staring at the calendar wondering what goes in Thursday's slot.

Put a number on your own hour. Not a nice number — the honest one, the rate you'd charge a client or the value of the next thing you'd do instead. Four hours a week at that rate is your current social budget. Most founders discover they're already "spending" more on social than any tool they've refused to buy.

This matters because it reframes every decision that follows. You're not deciding whether to start spending. You're deciding whether to keep paying in hours or start paying in dollars — and dollars are usually the cheaper currency, because hours are the one thing you can't buy more of.

## The four line items

Every social budget, at every size, breaks into the same four categories.

**1. Tools.** The software that holds the work: calendar, scheduler, AI for drafting, analytics, inbox. This is the smallest line and the one that compounds — it buys back hours every week, not once.

**2. Creative production.** Making the things: photo, video, design, editing. Either your time, a freelancer's, or an AI assist that gets you to a 70% draft.

**3. People.** Someone other than you doing the work — a part-time contractor, a VA handling the inbox, a creator producing content you couldn't make yourself.

**4. Amplification.** Paid distribution. Putting money behind posts that already proved they work organically.

The mistake nearly everyone makes is jumping straight to line 4 or line 3 — boosting posts or hiring help — while line 1 is still a spreadsheet and line 2 is still "I'll film something this weekend." That's paying to distribute content you haven't figured out how to make consistently.

## The order matters more than the total

Add the lines in sequence, and only move to the next one when the current one stops being your bottleneck.

**Stage 1 — $0.** Everything free. A free plan for scheduling, your phone's camera, the notes app for your idea bank. The goal here isn't growth, it's *proof you can keep a rhythm.* If you can't post twice a week for six weeks with zero spend, spending won't fix it — and you'll just have a paid version of an inconsistent account. Set one small, honest target first; [realistic goals](/blog/setting-realistic-social-media-goals-small-brand/) make the later budget decisions obvious.

**Stage 2 — tools.** The first money you spend should remove friction from the workflow you've proven you'll follow. Usually that's a real calendar, scheduling across platforms, AI drafting, and a single inbox so comments and DMs stop living in six apps. The test for this line item is simple: does it give back more hours than it costs in hours of your own rate? It almost always does, because this is the line that stops you doing the same manual job 40 times a year.

**Stage 3 — creative production.** Once the rhythm holds and the planning is fast, quality becomes the ceiling. This is where a freelance editor, a decent mic, a half-day photo shoot or a batch of AI-generated visuals earn their place. Note what you're buying: not "better content" in the abstract, but a specific bottleneck removed. "I can't edit video" is a bottleneck. "My posts should be better" isn't.

**Stage 4 — people and creators.** Now that there's a system to plug someone into, help actually helps. Before that, hiring just moves your confusion onto someone else's desk. If the gap is *volume of content you can't personally make*, [bringing in a creator](/blog/when-to-bring-in-a-creator-for-your-content-calendar/) is often cheaper than hiring; if the gap is *response time and admin*, a few hours a week of contractor time goes further.

**Stage 5 — amplification.** Last. Only put money behind posts that already earned attention without it. Organic performance is your cheapest possible ad test — a post that flopped organically will flop with $200 behind it, just more expensively.

## Three example splits (argue with them)

These are rules of thumb, not benchmarks — they're a starting point to react against, not data about what other companies do.

- **Bootstrapped, founder-run:** 100% tools. Everything else is your time. The whole budget is one or two subscriptions, and the win is hours returned.
- **Early traction, some money:** roughly 40% tools, 40% production, 20% amplification. Production is where quality steps up; amplification stays small and strictly post-validated.
- **Small team, revenue dependent on social:** roughly 20% tools, 30% production, 30% people, 20% amplification. Tools shrink as a *share* while growing in absolute terms.

Notice what no split includes: a line item for "we should also be on TikTok." Platform expansion isn't a budget decision, it's a capacity decision.

## What to cut first

When money gets tight, cut in this order:

1. **Overlapping tools.** Most small brands pay for three things that each do a third of the job. If you've ever counted [how many tools it takes to publish one post](/blog/how-many-tools-to-publish-one-post-tool-stack-cost/), you know the number is embarrassing — and separate AI subscriptions are usually the worst offender, because you're paying four vendors for the same category of help.
2. **Amplification without a winner.** Boosting mediocre posts is the easiest spend to pause and the hardest to miss.
3. **Anything you can't tie to a number.** Not a vanity number — an actual change in the metric you said mattered.

Cut tools *last*, not first. They're the line item that gives time back, and time is what you'll be shortest on the moment the budget shrinks.

## How to know a line item is earning its place

Review the budget on the same cadence you review performance — monthly is plenty. For each line, answer one question in writing: *what would get worse if we stopped paying this?*

If the answer is specific ("we'd go back to missing DMs for three days," "we'd lose a day a month to manual scheduling"), keep it. If the answer is a shrug, or a feeling, or "it's probably helping," you've found your cut. And if you can't tell whether social is contributing at all, fix that before adjusting any number — [tracking whether social actually drives sales](/blog/how-to-tell-if-social-media-drives-sales/) is a one-afternoon setup that makes every later budget call easier.
