# How Often Should You Post on Each Platform? A Realistic Frequency Guide

Search "how often should I post on Instagram" and you'll get five different numbers from five confident-sounding sources — one says once a day, another says three times a week, a third says it doesn't matter at all anymore. Ask the same question about LinkedIn, TikTok, and X, and the numbers multiply, contradict each other, and rarely agree on what "posting" even means (a Story? A Reel? A repost?).

The honest answer is that there isn't a universal number, because the platforms aren't playing the same game. What follows isn't a set of magic figures to hit — it's how each platform actually behaves, so you can set a frequency you can hold instead of one you saw in a screenshot.

## Why the advice is so contradictory

Most frequency advice gets confused because it mixes up three different questions: how often does the platform *reward* posting, how often do the accounts people study happen to post, and how often can a normal team actually sustain posting. Those numbers are wildly different from each other, and most "best practice" posts quietly average them into one figure that fits nobody.

A media brand with a content team of twelve posting five times a day on Instagram isn't a benchmark for a two-person startup — it's a different business with different resources. Treating their frequency as the target is how founders end up either burning out chasing a number that was never meant for them, or feeling like they're failing at a game with rules that don't apply to their situation.

So instead of one number, it helps to know what's actually true about how each platform distributes content — the mechanics, not the folklore.

## What each platform actually rewards

**Instagram** treats feed posts and Reels differently. Reels get pushed to non-followers through Explore and Reels surfaces, which is why accounts chasing reach post more Reels than static images — the algorithm is actively trying to find your content a wider audience there. Feed posts, by contrast, mostly reach people who already follow you, so posting more of them doesn't multiply reach the same way. The practical read: consistency matters more than volume, and a few well-made Reels a week usually outperforms a daily grid post nobody discovers.

**TikTok** is built around discovery by default — the For You page shows content to people who've never seen your account before, on every single post, not just your best ones. That structure genuinely rewards volume, because each post is a fresh roll of the dice for a new audience rather than a re-broadcast to the same followers. Accounts that post daily aren't doing it for vanity; they're doing it because TikTok's distribution model makes frequency a real lever, not just a habit.

**LinkedIn** runs the opposite way. The platform's feed leans toward fewer, more substantial posts from people it trusts to keep showing up with something worth reading — and LinkedIn audiences visibly punish anything that reads as filler. A sharp post twice a week reads as a professional showing up; the same account posting daily "thought leadership" without daily thoughts reads as noise, and engagement usually reflects that.

**X (Twitter)** rewards frequency because its feed is built around real-time scroll, not curated discovery — a post from six hours ago is ancient. Accounts that do well there post multiple times a day, not because the algorithm secretly loves it, but because the format is built for volume and half-life is short. If you're only posting once a day on X, you're mostly missing the audience that's online at other times.

**YouTube** (including Shorts) is closer to LinkedIn than TikTok: consistency of schedule matters more than raw count, because long-form content asks for a bigger investment from both you and the viewer. A channel that reliably posts every Tuesday builds an audience that shows up on Tuesdays. A channel that posts seven videos one week and none for a month trains nobody to expect anything.

**Facebook** rewards active, two-way pages — comments and shares more than post count — which means a slower cadence with genuine replies usually beats a faster one that's purely broadcast.

## The trade-off nobody puts on the infographic

Here's the tension every frequency guide dodges: the platforms that reward volume (TikTok, X) are also the ones where a mediocre post barely costs you anything, because it's one post among your fifty that month. The platforms that reward restraint (LinkedIn, YouTube) are the ones where a mediocre post costs you more, because it's one of your two posts that week and it's more visible by default.

That means the right question per platform isn't just "how often," it's "how much does a weak post cost me here." On TikTok, post more and let the algorithm surface your best ones. On LinkedIn, post less and make each one earn its slot.

## Finding your actual number

None of this tells you your number — it tells you the shape of the trade-off you're making on each platform. Your actual number comes from a much simpler test: pick a frequency you could sustain through a bad month, not just a good one. Launch week enthusiasm produces a cadence nobody keeps in November. If daily posting on Instagram means skipping weeks when things get busy, three times a week that never lapses will beat it over a year, on almost every platform that isn't TikTok.

Start lower than you think you should, hold it for a month without missing, and raise it only once the lower number feels easy — not aspirational.

## Managing different cadences without losing track

The real difficulty isn't picking a number for one platform — it's holding five different numbers at once without losing track of which platform is overdue and which just posted. A content calendar that shows all your channels side by side, rather than five separate apps or a spreadsheet with color codes, is what makes "three times a week on LinkedIn, daily on TikTok, twice a week on Instagram" something you can actually see and hold — instead of a plan that only exists in your head until it quietly slips.
