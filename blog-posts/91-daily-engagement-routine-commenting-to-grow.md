You've been posting consistently for two months. The calendar is full, the captions are decent, the visuals are on brand. And the numbers have barely moved.

The instinct is to post more. Usually the better move is to post the same amount and spend twenty minutes a day in other people's comment sections.

This is the part of social media that never makes it onto a content calendar, which is exactly why most small brands skip it. It has no publish button and nothing to approve. It is also, for an account without an existing audience, often the fastest-working lever available.

## Publishing is a broadcast. Commenting is a door.

When you publish, you're asking an algorithm to show your post to people who already follow you, plus a few who might. Your reach is capped by the audience you already have — which is the problem you're trying to solve.

When you comment on someone else's post, you're borrowing an audience that already gathered. Your name sits under a post that people are actively reading, next to a thought that either earns a click on your profile or doesn't. Nothing has to be distributed for that to happen.

There's a second effect that matters more over time. Replying to someone's post is the cheapest possible way to start a relationship with a peer, a potential partner, or a customer — and relationships are what eventually produce the things you can't manufacture: shares, mentions, introductions, [cross-promotions](/blog/brand-partnership-cross-promotion-small-brands/).

## Build the list before you build the habit

The reason most people's engagement effort fizzles is that each session starts with "whose posts should I look at?" Twenty minutes evaporates in the feed, you leave four comments on whatever the algorithm served you, and none of it compounds.

Fix that once by writing a list of 30 accounts. Four categories, roughly:

- **Potential customers** — people who fit your ICP and post publicly. For most B2B founders this is the biggest group.
- **Peers** — other founders or brands at a similar stage, not competitors you'd hate to help. Reciprocity here is real and it's mutual.
- **Adjacent businesses** — people who serve your customer without competing. The agency to your software, the photographer to your venue.
- **Voices your customer already follows** — the handful of bigger accounts whose comment sections are where your audience congregates.

Keep it in whatever you already use — a saved list on X, an Instagram "close friends"-style collection, a note, a column in your planning doc. The format matters far less than the fact that the question "who?" is answered before you start.

Refresh it monthly. Drop accounts that have gone quiet, add anyone whose post you found yourself genuinely wanting to reply to.

## The routine

Twenty minutes, once a day, on the platform where your audience actually is. If you're still deciding where that is, start with [the platform question](/blog/which-social-platforms-should-your-brand-be-on/) rather than spreading this across five apps.

**Minutes 0–5: your own house.** Reply to every comment and DM on your recent posts first. Replies you owe people outrank comments you want to leave. If your comments and messages are scattered across platforms, pulling them into [one inbox](/blog/unified-inbox-social-comments-dms-workflow/) is what makes five minutes enough.

**Minutes 5–17: the list.** Work down your 30 accounts, not the feed. Leave somewhere between five and ten real comments. You will not get to all 30 in a day, and you shouldn't try — rotate.

**Minutes 17–20: capture.** Anything you noticed that could become a post goes straight into your idea bank. Comment sections are the most honest source of content ideas you have: the same question asked three times this week is next Tuesday's post.

Then close the app. The hardest part of this routine isn't starting it, it's stopping it — "engagement" is a very comfortable place to spend two hours doing nothing measurable.

## What a comment worth leaving looks like

The bar is simple: would this comment be worth reading if your name weren't attached to it?

That rules out "Great post!", the emoji stack, and the thinly disguised ad. It leaves four things that work:

1. **Add a case they didn't cover.** "This holds until you're shipping internationally — then the maths changes because…" You're extending the post, not grading it.
2. **Bring the specific.** They said something general; you've actually done it. Name the number, the tool, the timeline. Specificity is the whole signal.
3. **Disagree well.** Politely, with a reason, no dunking. A well-argued disagreement is the single most profile-click-worthy comment type there is — and the riskiest to get wrong in tone.
4. **Ask the real follow-up question.** Not for engagement bait, because you want the answer. Authors reply to those.

Write like a person with a point of view, not a brand with a positioning statement. And don't pitch. If the comment is good, the profile does the pitching — which is why it's worth making sure [your bio actually converts](/blog/social-media-profile-bio-optimization/) before you drive traffic to it.

## Where the twenty minutes comes from

Nobody has a spare twenty minutes. You get it by moving publishing off the daily path.

If you're writing and posting one post at a time, every day contains a small creative emergency and engagement is the first thing cut. If the month is drafted and queued ahead of time, the daily job shrinks to conversations. That's the practical argument for [planning and scheduling in batches](/blog/batching-content-vs-posting-on-the-fly/): not tidiness, but the hour it hands back.

That's the trade Trendly is built around — use AI across [strategy, drafting, and the calendar](/product/content-calendar/) so the publishing work happens in one sitting, then spend the reclaimed daily time on the part no tool can do for you. An AI can draft a caption. It cannot credibly represent you in a stranger's comment section, and you shouldn't want it to.

## How to tell if it's working

Not follower count — too slow and too noisy. Watch these instead, weekly:

- **Profile visits** (most platforms report this). This is the direct effect of commenting, and it moves within days.
- **Inbound DMs and replies from people you don't know.** The clearest early signal.
- **Where new followers came from**, if your platform shows discovery sources at all.
- **Named mentions and tags** from accounts on your list, over months rather than weeks.

Give it four weeks before judging, and treat it as a habit rather than a campaign. The uncomfortable truth about this tactic is that it has no ceiling and no shortcut: it works roughly in proportion to how much you actually care about the conversations you're joining.
