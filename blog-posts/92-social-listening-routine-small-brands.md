# Nobody Tagged You: A Social Listening Routine for Small Brands

Your notifications tab is a flattering, incomplete picture. It shows you the people who remembered to tag you, spelled your name right, and were feeling generous enough to @ you in public.

Everyone else just talks. They mention your product in a comment under someone else's post. They screenshot your pricing page into a group chat. They ask "has anyone tried [your category]?" on Reddit and get three recommendations that aren't you. They write "the one with the orange logo" instead of your handle.

None of that reaches you. And that's usually where the useful stuff lives — the objections, the comparisons, the misunderstandings, the near-misses. Social listening is just the habit of going to find it.

Here's a routine small enough that a solo founder can actually keep it.

## Decide what you're listening for

"Monitor everything" is how listening dies in week two. Pick five buckets and write the actual search terms down somewhere you'll reuse them.

**Your brand, misspelled.** Your name without the @, your name with a typo, your product name, your founder's name. If your brand is a common word, pair it with a qualifier — your category, "app", "tool".

**Your category, as a question.** "best tool for X", "how do you handle X", "any recommendations for X". These are people shopping out loud. They rarely tag anyone.

**Your competitors.** Not to obsess — to find the people saying "I like it but I wish it did Y." Y is your content for next week. (That's a different job from a full [competitor content audit](/blog/competitor-content-audit-what-to-actually-learn/), which looks at what they post; this is about what their customers say back.)

**Your customers' real problem, in their words.** Not "inventory management software" but "I keep overselling stock." The vocabulary you find here is better than any keyword tool will give you.

**Your own past claims.** If you said something notable publicly, search for it. People quote and argue with brands without tagging them constantly.

Five buckets, maybe fifteen search strings total. That's the whole setup.

## The 30-minute weekly pass

Put it on the calendar once a week. Same slot every week. Thirty minutes, timer on.

**Minutes 1–10: run your searches.** Native search on each platform you care about — Instagram, LinkedIn, X, TikTok, YouTube comments. Google with `site:reddit.com` plus your category. If you sell anything with reviews, your own review pages count.

**Minutes 11–20: read the comment sections you're not in.** Pick two or three posts from your category this week that did well — a competitor's, a creator's, a news post. Don't read the post. Read the replies. This is where people say what they actually think, because they're talking to each other and not to a brand.

**Minutes 21–30: capture, don't react.** Paste anything useful into one place — a note, a doc, your idea bank. One line each: what they said, where, and what it tells you. You are collecting raw material, not fixing anything yet.

Resist the urge to reply to everything during the pass. Reacting and collecting are different modes, and mixing them means you'll spend all thirty minutes writing a single careful response to one stranger.

## Sort what you heard into three piles

At the end of the pass, everything you captured goes into one of three piles.

**Reply now.** Someone asked a question you can answer, misunderstood something, or recommended you to someone else. These are time-sensitive and high-value. Handle them the same way you'd handle a tagged comment — fast, human, no copy-paste. If it's a complaint or a public pile-on, the rules in our [negative comments playbook](/blog/how-to-handle-negative-comments-social-media/) apply here too.

**Make content.** Every repeated question is a post. Every "I wish it did Y" is a post. Every misunderstanding is a post, and usually your highest-performing one, because you're correcting something people already believe. This feeds the same engine as [turning customer questions into content](/blog/turning-customer-questions-into-a-month-of-content/) — listening just widens the intake.

**Tell someone internally.** Pricing confusion, a broken onboarding step, a feature people assume you have. This is the pile founders under-use. Social listening is cheap product research disguised as marketing work.

## Unlinked mentions are warm leads, not just trivia

When someone mentions you without tagging you, they're usually mid-decision — or they've already recommended you to a friend. Both are worth five minutes.

The move is light. Show up, add something useful, don't sell. "Founder here — happy to answer anything" is enough. If someone recommended you, thank them in public where the recommendation happened, not in a DM where nobody sees it.

Two rules keep this from getting weird. Don't argue with people who don't like you — the audience is the third party reading, and arguing loses them. And don't show up in every thread in your category; being the brand that's everywhere is a worse look than being absent.

The best unlinked mentions, with permission, become [user-generated content](/blog/ugc-system-customer-content-small-brands/). A real sentence from a real customer beats anything you'd write about yourself.

## Listening makes your AI drafts better

This is the part people miss. If you use AI to write — and you should — the quality ceiling is set by what you feed it, not by which model you pick.

An AI brief built from listening notes reads completely differently than one built from your own assumptions. "Write a post about our scheduling feature" produces a generic post. "Write a post answering this objection, in this person's words: 'I don't want another tool, I already have four'" produces something specific enough to land.

## Make it small enough to survive a bad week

A listening routine fails for the same reason every other content habit fails: it's too big on day one.

Start with three searches, one platform, fifteen minutes. Do it four weeks in a row before adding anything. If you miss a week, run it the next week — there's no backlog to clear, because old conversations stop mattering anyway.

And keep it next to your other recurring work. If you already run a [daily engagement routine](/blog/daily-engagement-routine-commenting-to-grow/), bolt listening onto the front of it once a week: five minutes of searching before you start commenting, so you're commenting on the right things.

The goal isn't to catch every mention. It's to stop being the last person to know what your market thinks of you.
