# The Social Media Report Your Boss Actually Wants to Read

At some point, someone above you — a boss, a co-founder, a client, an investor on a monthly call — is going to ask "how's social media going?" If your honest answer involves opening four different apps, screenshotting a handful of charts, and pasting them into a slide at 11pm the night before, you already know why that question makes you tense up. The problem usually isn't that the work isn't going well. It's that nobody has a report ready to answer the question in under two minutes.

Here's a lighter way to do it — one that takes less time to produce and gets read all the way through, because it answers the three questions the person on the other end actually has.

## The three questions behind "how's it going"

Nobody asking about social media wants a metrics dump. They're really asking three things, in this order:

1. **Is this working?** — a yes/no signal, not twelve charts.
2. **What did you do about it?** — proof there's a person steering, not just a dashboard being watched.
3. **What happens next?** — so the conversation ends with a decision, not a shrug.

A report that answers those three things in three short sections beats a report with more data in it every time. The goal isn't to prove you did a lot of work. It's to let the reader form an opinion fast and move on with their day.

## What to leave out

Before the template, the shorter list: leave out anything you'd only include to look busy. Impressions without context, a screenshot of every post from the month, raw follower count with no trend line, any number you can't explain in one sentence if asked. If a metric doesn't change what happens next, it's decoration — and decoration is exactly what turns a report into something people skim past. (If you're not sure which numbers clear that bar, [vanity metrics vs. the numbers that actually move the needle](/blog/social-metrics-that-actually-move-the-needle/) is a good gut check before you build the first version of this.)

## A format that fits on one screen

**1. The headline, one line.**
Not "here's our social media update" — an actual verdict. "Engagement is up 22% since we switched to more behind-the-scenes content" or "Reach is flat, but link clicks from Reels are our best channel this month." Whoever reads only this line should still walk away knowing the state of things.

**2. Three numbers, each with a one-sentence why.**
Pick the three that actually moved: maybe it's saves, profile visits, and click-through rate. For each, write the number, last month's number next to it, and one sentence on the likely cause. "Saves: 340, up from 210 — the how-to carousel format is outperforming everything else we post." A number without a cause is just trivia; the cause is what makes it useful to someone deciding what to fund next.

**3. What worked, in one or two examples.**
Name the specific post, not the category. "The founder-story reel on the 14th" beats "our video content." Specifics are what let a boss or client picture the thing, and they're what you'll want to remember when you're planning next month anyway.

**4. What's next, as a decision, not a status update.**
"We're doubling down on carousels and testing one Reel a week on LinkedIn" is a plan. "We'll keep posting" is not a sentence anyone needed a report to deliver. This section is also where you ask for anything you actually need — budget, a faster approval turnaround, access to a customer story — while you have their attention.

That's the whole report. Four sections, most of them one or two sentences, no screenshots required unless one post is worth showing off.

## How often to send it

Monthly is enough for almost everyone reading this — weekly is usually reporting on noise, since most social metrics need a few weeks to show a real trend. The exception is a live launch or campaign, where a quick mid-point check-in (just the headline line, nothing else) keeps whoever's watching from feeling like they're waiting in the dark. Outside of that, pick one date a month, put it on the calendar, and send it whether or not anything dramatic happened — a quiet month reported on time builds more trust than a dramatic month reported late.

## Where the AI shortcut actually helps

The tedious part of this report has never been the thinking — it's re-opening the analytics tab, copying four numbers, and finding last month's version to compare against. That's exactly the kind of drafting AI is good at and you shouldn't have to be: pull last month's numbers next to this month's, and it can draft the headline line and the three-number section in your voice, leaving you to fill in the "what's next" decision yourself since that part genuinely needs a human. The report itself won't make the results better. But a report that gets read all the way through is what turns "how's social going?" from a question you dread into one you're ready for — and that's worth more to how the work gets funded than any single chart.
