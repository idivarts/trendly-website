# How to Run a Social Media Giveaway That Grows the Right Audience

There's a particular kind of disappointment that follows a successful giveaway. The post does numbers you've never seen. Your follower count jumps in a way that feels like something finally worked. Then you announce the winner, post something normal the next week, and engagement is worse than before you started. A chunk of the new followers quietly leave. The ones who stay never buy anything.

The giveaway didn't fail. It did exactly what you designed it to do: reward people for wanting a free thing. Making it do something more useful means changing the design — and most of that work happens before you write a word of the caption.

## Decide what the giveaway is actually for

"Grow our following" is not a goal. It's a number that can go up without anything good happening underneath it. Pick one of these instead, and only one:

- **Reach people who don't follow you yet.** The mechanic should push sharing — stories, reposts, tagging — because that's what gets you into new feeds.
- **Build an owned list.** Entry happens off-platform: a form, an email signup, a waitlist. Fewer entries, far more durable.
- **Collect content and proof.** Entry is "post a photo using the product and tag us," and you get [user-generated content](/blog/ugc-system-customer-content-small-brands) you can use for months.
- **Launch a specific product.** The prize *is* the new thing, and the giveaway is really an awareness campaign for it.

Each goal implies a different prize, entry step, and definition of success. Choosing two means optimizing for neither.

## Pick a prize only your real customer would want

This is the single decision that determines who shows up.

An iPad attracts everyone. "Everyone" is not your audience — it's serial giveaway enterers who follow accounts to qualify and unfollow when the winner is announced. You pay for the prize and the reach, and keep almost none of the audience.

A prize that's useless to someone outside your category does the filtering for you. A full set of your own product. A year of your subscription. A bundle built around a problem only your customer has. If a prize makes a stranger shrug and scroll past, that's the prize working correctly.

To make it feel bigger without making it broader, partner with a complementary brand your customer already uses — two relevant audiences, one prize pool. Agree upfront on who posts what, who holds the entry data, and who ships.

## Keep the entry mechanic to one action

Every extra step cuts entries, and most extra steps don't improve quality — they just make the caption longer. Pick one primary action tied to your goal: comment, share to stories, or sign up at a link. If you need a second, make it optional and worth something ("tag a friend for a bonus entry"), not mandatory.

Then write the rules so a tired person on a phone can follow them: deadline with a timezone, who can enter, what they win, when you'll announce. If your rules need a second read, you'll spend the whole campaign answering the same question in DMs.

## Read the platform's promotion rules before you write the caption

Every major platform has its own contest rules, and they change. Two are consistent enough to plan around: **don't build mechanics that require people to break platform rules** (asking entrants to create extra accounts puts both of you at risk of enforcement), and **you're responsible for your own promotion** — the platform isn't administering it, isn't liable for it, and generally requires you to say so, along with a statement that eligibility, age limits, and territory are on you.

Giveaways are regulated by local law in many places too, and the specifics depend on where you and your entrants are. Anything with a meaningful prize value, spanning multiple countries, or resembling a lottery should be checked by someone qualified in that jurisdiction. This is one of the few corners of social media where "we'll figure it out" has real consequences.

## Plan it as a campaign, not a post

A giveaway that lives in one post gets seen by whoever happens to be online that afternoon. Put it on the calendar as a short campaign and you reach the same audience four or five times without being annoying. A workable shape:

1. **Announce.** The prize, the one action, the deadline. Clear and visual.
2. **Explain or show.** A second angle 2–3 days later — why this prize, who it's for. This reaches everyone who scrolled past the first one.
3. **Mid-campaign nudge.** Share an entry you loved, or answer the question you keep getting in the comments.
4. **Last call.** 24 hours out. Consistently pulls entries from people who meant to and forgot.
5. **Winner + what's next.** Announce, thank everyone, and give the new followers a reason to stay: a discount for non-winners, an early look, an invitation to the list.

Post five is where most giveaways are quietly lost. It's your only chance to turn a crowd that's paying attention into an audience that sticks — treat it like the [launch post](/blog/plan-a-product-launch-across-social-channels) it actually is.

## Prepare for the entry flood

A giveaway generates more inbound messages in three days than your account normally sees in a month, and most of it is the same four questions.

So write your replies before you launch. "When do you announce?" "Can I enter from X country?" "Do I have to follow?" "Did I win?" Saved answers let you clear two hundred comments in twenty minutes instead of abandoning the thread — and an abandoned thread under your best post of the quarter is a bad look.

Working out of [one inbox for every platform](/blog/unified-inbox-social-comments-dms-workflow) matters more than usual here, because entries arrive as comments on one network and DMs on another. If your entry step is a comment, automation that replies to each entrant — confirming they're in, sending the link — handles volume you'd otherwise eat manually. It's the same [comment-to-DM mechanic](/blog/comment-to-cart-automation-turning-comments-into-customers) that works for product drops.

## Pick the winner in a way you can defend

Use a tool that picks randomly from the qualifying entries, and record your screen while it does. When someone asks whether it was rigged — and on a big enough giveaway, someone always does — you want a thirty-second clip, not an argument.

Announce publicly on the schedule you promised, contact the winner privately, and put a claim deadline in the original rules so an unresponsive winner doesn't strand the campaign. Never ask for payment details to release a prize — that's what every giveaway scam does.

## Measure it 30 days later, not the next morning

The morning-after numbers look great and tell you nothing. The ones that matter arrive about a month out:

- **Followers retained**, not followers gained. Compare your count the day after the announcement to your count 30 days later. The gap was never real.
- **Engagement on your next three normal posts.** If a bigger audience is engaging less, you bought reach from people who don't care.
- **Entries that became something** — list signups, first orders, a usable piece of UGC. One of those beats a thousand comments, and it's the only figure that connects the giveaway to [actual sales](/blog/how-to-tell-if-social-media-drives-sales).

Write those three down somewhere you'll find them next time. Two giveaways compared honestly teach you more about your audience than a year of guessing.

## Where this fits in a normal workflow

Giveaways get run badly not because founders don't know better, but because a five-post campaign plus a rules block plus an inbox surge is a lot to stand up in the week you decided to do it. It's far more manageable when the pieces live together: the five posts drafted from one prompt and dropped onto the [content calendar](/product/content-calendar) with the deadline and timezone already in them, a teammate reviewing the rules copy before it ships rather than after, and entry replies handled in the same inbox as everything else.

Design the whole thing for the fifty people who were always going to be your customers, not the five thousand who want an iPad. The number will be smaller. Everything after it will be better.
