# Should You Post Your Prices? How to Talk About Money on Social

Someone comments "how much?" under your post. You reply "DM us for pricing!" They don't DM. The comment sits there, and the next person who was about to ask the same question reads your reply, decides it'll be a negotiation, and scrolls on.

Price is the one thing almost every buyer wants to know and the one thing most brands are strangely coy about on social. There are real reasons to hold the number back — but most founders are holding it back by default rather than by decision, and the default costs them the buyers who would have said yes.

## First, decide whether the number should be public

This isn't a philosophy question. It depends on three things.

**Is your price roughly fixed?** If you sell a product, a subscription, or a packaged service at a published rate, there is almost no argument for hiding it. The number is already on your website. Repeating it on social costs you nothing and saves every prospect a step.

**Does your price vary by scope?** Custom work — a build, a retainer, a wedding, a renovation — genuinely can't be reduced to one number. But "it varies" is not the same as "it's unknowable." You can almost always publish a floor ("projects start at $4,000") or a range with the variables named.

**Are you deliberately premium?** Some brands hide price because being asked is part of the positioning. That's a legitimate strategy, but it only works if everything else — the work, the photography, the waitlist — supports it. If you're hiding a $200 price point behind "DM for details," you're not creating mystique. You're creating friction.

For most founders and small teams, the honest answer is: your price should be public, at least as a starting number.

## What "DM us for pricing" actually does

It feels like qualification. It mostly does three other things.

It **filters by effort, not by fit.** The person willing to open a DM and wait for a reply isn't the person with the biggest budget. It's the person with the most free time. You lose the busy, ready-to-buy buyer and keep the browser.

It **signals negotiation.** A hidden price tells people the number depends on who's asking — which some read as "they'll charge what they think I can pay." That's the opposite of the trust the previous nine posts were building.

It **moves the conversation off the post.** Comments are public and compounding: one answered question educates everyone who reads it later. A DM answers one person, privately, once.

If you can't publish a number, at least publish a band in the reply: *"Most projects land between $3k and $8k depending on scope — happy to walk through yours."* That answers enough to keep the self-disqualifiers out and the real buyers in.

## How to post about price without sounding defensive

The common failure isn't hiding the number. It's announcing the number apologetically, or over-justifying it. Four formats that work:

**The floor post.** State the starting price and what's in it. No hedging, no "we know it's an investment." *"Our audit starts at $1,200. That's two weeks, a 20-page findings doc, and a call to walk through it."* Clear, finishable, scannable.

**The "what it replaces" post.** Price is only ever judged against an alternative, so name the alternative yourself. If your $79 plan replaces a scheduler, a design subscription, and three AI tools, say that, and say what those cost. You're not arguing the price is small. You're arguing the comparison set is wrong.

**The breakdown post.** Carousels are built for this. One slide per line item — what you actually do, what it takes, what it costs. People don't object to a price; they object to a price they can't account for. Showing the parts answers the objection before it's raised.

**The "who this isn't for" post.** The most underused pricing post on social. *"If you need this done for under $500, we're not the right fit — here's what we'd do in your position instead."* It costs you nothing (that buyer was never converting) and it does more for credibility than any testimonial.

Notice none of these is a discount.

## Handling the four replies you'll get

**"How much?"** Answer in the comment with a number or a band, then offer the next step. Don't make them ask twice.

**"That's expensive."** Don't justify. Reframe to the unit that matters — per month, per use, per deliverable — or ask what they're comparing it to. Often the comparison is a product doing half the job, and naming that is the whole conversation.

**"Do you have anything cheaper?"** If you have a lower tier, this is a gift. If you don't, say no plainly and offer a smaller first step: an audit, a single deliverable, a trial.

**Silence.** The most common one. People read your price post, decide, and say nothing. This is why price posts look like they underperform — they rarely get comments, and they do their work weeks later on a call where the prospect already knows the number.

## Discounts: use them with an end date and a reason

Discounting isn't a sin, but an unexplained discount tells people your real price was fiction. Two rules.

**Always give the reason.** Launch, anniversary, end of quarter, founding customers, a clearing-the-shelf sale. A reason protects the regular price. "20% off because it's our first birthday" doesn't reprice you. "20% off" repeated monthly does.

**Always give the end date.** Not for urgency theatre — so the discount ends. An offer with no deadline becomes the new price, and now you've cut your margin permanently to win customers who'd have paid full price.

And keep a count. If more than roughly one in five of your promotional posts is a discount, price isn't your lever anymore — it's your crutch.

## Where price fits in the mix

Pricing content isn't a campaign. It's a recurring slot. A workable rhythm for a small brand: a price-or-offer post roughly every two weeks, one objection-handling post a month, and the number itself repeated wherever it's relevant — in a caption, in a comment, in the bio link.

The reason to schedule it rather than post it when you feel brave is that pricing posts are the ones founders quietly skip. They feel pushy in a way an educational post doesn't. Put them in the calendar in advance and they stop being a decision you have to make on the day.

## Start with the comment you keep retyping

Go find the last five times someone asked what something costs and look at what you replied. If it was "DM us," you already know your answer is repeatable — you've been repeating it. Write it once, publicly, as a post with the number in it.

The buyers who were going to leave over the price were always going to leave. The only thing hiding it changes is how long they take to go, and how many ready buyers you lose on the way.
