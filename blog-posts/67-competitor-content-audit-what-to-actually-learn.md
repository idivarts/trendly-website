# The Competitor Content Audit: What to Actually Learn From Brands You Follow

Most competitor research looks like this: you open Instagram, scroll a rival's grid, screenshot a Reel that did well, and drop it in a "content ideas" folder you'll never open again. That's not an audit. It's a highlight reel with no context — you don't know why the post worked, whether it was a fluke, or whether it would even work for your audience.

A real competitor content audit isn't about copying. It's about pattern-spotting: what formats a category rewards, what a rival's audience actually responds to versus what the brand *thinks* is working, and where there's a gap nobody in your space has filled yet. Done well, it takes an afternoon and gives you a list of testable ideas — not a vague sense that "we should probably do more video."

Here's a framework you can run in about two hours, plus how to turn the findings into something that lands on your calendar instead of in a forgotten folder.

## Start by picking the right competitors — not just the obvious ones

Most brands audit the same two or three direct competitors every time, which means you only ever see the ideas already circulating inside your own category. Widen the list on purpose.

- **2-3 direct competitors** — same product, same audience, roughly your size.
- **1-2 aspirational brands** — bigger players you'd like to resemble in three years. They usually have more resources but also more constraints (legal review, brand guidelines) that make some of their choices less useful to copy.
- **1-2 adjacent brands** — different product, same audience. A skincare brand and a supplements brand often share a customer even though they don't compete. Adjacent brands are where you find formats your direct competitors haven't tried yet.

If your list is all direct competitors, you'll end up with ideas everyone in your niche already has. The adjacent brands are usually where the actual gaps show up.

## Look at the grid before you look at any single post

Before you zoom in on individual posts, scroll the last 20-30 posts on each account and just look at the pattern. You're answering four questions:

1. **What's the format mix?** Rough split of static posts, carousels, Reels/short video, and Stories highlights. A brand that's gone 80% video in the last two months has learned something about their audience that a single viral Reel doesn't tell you.
2. **What's the posting rhythm?** Daily, a few times a week, or bursty around launches? Rhythm tells you as much about their team's capacity as their strategy — a rhythm that looks unsustainable for a 20-person team probably isn't a fair benchmark for a 2-person one.
3. **What repeats?** Look for a recurring series — a weekly Q&A, a "behind the scenes" slot, a recurring meme format. Recurring series are usually the highest-signal thing on a grid, because a brand doesn't keep running a format that flops.
4. **What's missing?** This is the most useful question and the one people skip. If nobody in your category is doing founder-led content, or nobody is showing the product being used (only the product on a shelf), that gap is often more valuable than anything you'll find by copying what's already crowded.

## Then go post-by-post, but only on the outliers

Don't catalogue every post — that's how audits turn into a spreadsheet nobody reads. Instead, sort by engagement (most platforms let you view a grid by likes, or you can eyeball it) and pull the 5-8 posts that clearly over- or under-performed relative to the account's usual range.

For each outlier, note:

- **The hook** — first line of caption, or first 2 seconds of video. What's the pattern-interrupt?
- **The format** — talking head, text-on-screen, carousel with a specific slide structure, UGC repost?
- **The angle** — is it educational, funny, controversial, aspirational, a customer story?
- **The comments** — this is the part most audits skip entirely. Comments tell you what the audience actually cared about, which is sometimes completely different from what the caption was trying to sell. A post about a new feature that gets flooded with "where can I buy this" comments is telling you something about intent, not about the feature.

Do the same for the *underperformers* if you can find any — a rival's worst posts are often just as instructive, especially if there's a clear pattern (every listicle-style caption flops, every talking-head video does fine).

## What to actually copy, and what to leave alone

This is where most competitor research goes wrong — it either copies too literally (same joke, same product shot, same caption structure) or gets dismissed entirely because "our brand is different." Neither is right.

**Safe to borrow:**
- Formats and structures (carousel with a hook slide, then three tips, then a CTA slide)
- Posting cadence and rhythm, adapted to your team's actual capacity
- Topics and angles that are working across *multiple* brands in your space, not just one — that's a signal of audience demand, not one brand's lucky post
- Question types that generate comments, adapted to your own product

**Don't copy directly:**
- Specific jokes, memes, or cultural references — these age in days and read as stale the moment you post them a week later
- Visual identity — fonts, colors, a very recognizable set format
- Anything that only works because of *that* brand's specific reputation or history (an edgy joke lands differently from an established brand than from a new one)

The goal isn't to have a rival's exact playbook. It's to walk away with a short list of formats and angles you're confident will resonate with *your* audience, informed by evidence instead of a gut feeling.

## Turn it into calendar slots, not a swipe file

The audit is only useful if it changes what gets scheduled. After each round, pull 3-5 concrete ideas and write them as actual content briefs — not "do more video" but "3-slide carousel: the mistake we made before we fixed X, hook is the mistake, last slide is the fix." Vague takeaways die in a notes app. Specific briefs make it onto the calendar.

This is also a good moment to check your own content pillars — if every idea from the audit falls into a pillar you're already covering, you haven't actually found a gap. If an idea doesn't fit any existing pillar, that's worth a second look: either it's a pillar you're missing, or it's a format fit for a rival's brand and not yours.

If you're planning inside Trendly, this is a natural fit for the AI chat: paste in what you noticed (formats, hooks, gaps) and ask it to draft calendar-ready briefs in your brand voice, rather than in whatever tone the post you're inspired by used. That keeps the audit from turning into a copy-paste job — you're borrowing the *pattern*, not the *brand*.

## How often to actually do this

Once a quarter is usually the right cadence for a small team — often enough to catch shifting formats (a new video style spreading through a category), rare enough that it doesn't eat a week every month. If you're in a fast-moving category (anything heavily driven by short-form video trends) monthly is more realistic, but keep it to the same tight two-hour framework so it doesn't balloon into a research project that never ships an actual post.

The point of a competitor audit was never to know what everyone else is doing for its own sake. It's to walk away with a shorter list of formats worth testing and a clearer sense of the gap nobody else in your space has filled yet — then get it on the calendar before the idea goes stale.
